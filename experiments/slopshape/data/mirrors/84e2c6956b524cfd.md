# "Price Should Follow Value, Not Your Costs": Nicolas Dessaigne on Pricing, Billing, and Open Source

Few founders have lived through the messy middle of usage-based pricing quite like Nicolas Dessaigne. As co-founder and former CEO of Algolia, he helped turn a small search engine for mobile apps into the search and discovery API behind thousands of websites and apps, handling billions of queries every month. Along the way, Algolia moved from simple fixed plans to a pricing model built on usage: records indexed and search operations performed.

That change sounds clean on a slide. In practice, it touched every part of the company, from sales and finance to the tangle of code that turned usage data into invoices. We asked Nicolas what went right, what went wrong, and what he'd tell founders today.

---

**Let's start at the beginning. How did you pick your first plans?**

Honestly, we looked at what other developer tools were doing and made an educated guess: a few tiers, each with a monthly price and a limit on records and operations. Simple was the right call. When you have ten customers, people need to understand what they're buying in about five seconds.

The problem is that those first plans stick around much longer than you expect. You think you'll revisit them in six months, and then three years later you're still supporting them.

**Why did you decide to build billing in-house?**

Because it looked easy. That's the trap. At the start, billing is a cron job that counts usage and sends a number to Stripe. Any engineer can write that in a week. And as an engineering-led company, our reflex was to build things ourselves. We wanted control, and we didn't see anything on the market that matched how we wanted to charge.

So we wrote it. It worked. And for a while nobody thought about it again.

**You've mentioned a pricing experiment that didn't go the way you hoped. What happened?**

At one point we tried to tie our prices more closely to what things cost us to run. Our logic was very rational from the inside: certain operations were expensive for our infrastructure, so we should charge more for them. We restructured some of the pricing around that.

Customers hated it. Not because it was expensive, exactly, but because it didn't make sense from where they sat. They didn't care about our server costs. They cared about what search did for their business: conversions, engagement, happy users. We were asking them to understand our architecture in order to predict their bill. That's a terrible experience.

We rolled a lot of it back. It was a painful but very useful lesson.

**What was the lesson, in a sentence?**

Price should follow the value your customer gets, not your internal costs. Your costs set the floor, and you have to make sure your margins work. But the metric you charge on should be something the customer recognizes as value. If they grow and succeed, your revenue should grow with them, and they should feel good about that, not surprised by it.

**Let's go back to the homegrown billing system. When did it start to hurt?**

It crept up on us. First, finance needed things. They wanted proper revenue recognition, invoices that matched contracts, credit notes, taxes in different countries. Then sales started signing custom deals with commitments, discounts, and overage rates. Every one of those needed a special case in the code.

Then there were grandfathered plans. Every time we changed pricing, we kept existing customers on their old plans, which is the right thing to do for trust. But it meant we were running five, six, seven generations of pricing at the same time, and the billing logic had to handle all of them correctly.

And then fundraising. When you raise a big round, investors want to understand your revenue deeply: net dollar retention, expansion by cohort, usage trends. Suddenly your billing data isn't just for invoices. It's the source of truth for how you present the company. If that data is messy, you have a real problem.

**So what's your advice to founders deciding whether to build or buy?**

Don't build billing unless billing is your product. I know it's tempting, especially for technical founders. It looks simple and you feel like you'll have more flexibility. But the complexity doesn't come from the first version. It comes from year two, year three, year five, when finance, sales, and investors all have requirements you can't imagine today.

The other piece of advice is to pick something that won't box you in. You will change your pricing. Probably many times. Whatever you choose should let you experiment without a rewrite every time.

**Why do you think open source matters for something like billing?**

Billing is extremely sensitive. It's your revenue, and it's your customers' trust. With open source, you can see exactly how things are calculated. You can host it yourself if you need to. You can extend it when your model is unusual, which, with usage-based pricing, it almost always is. You're not waiting on a vendor's roadmap to launch a new plan.

It also removes the lock-in fear. Migrating billing systems is one of the scariest projects a company can take on.

**And how do open-core companies actually make money if the code is free?**

The core is free and that's what drives adoption. Developers try it, trust it, and build on it. Then as companies grow, they need things that matter at scale: a managed cloud so they don't have to run it themselves, enterprise features around security and permissions, advanced integrations, support with real guarantees. That's where they pay.

The best open-core companies are very honest about that line. The free version has to be genuinely useful, not a crippled demo. Do that well, and your community becomes your best sales channel.
