# What Are Coding Bootcamps? (Part 1 of 2)

Over the past decade, coding bootcamps have gone from a novel experiment to a well-established pathway into the tech industry. They promise something that sounds almost too good to be true: learn to code in a matter of months and land a well-paying job as a software developer. For thousands of students, that promise has become reality.

But what exactly are coding bootcamps, where did they come from, and how do they compare with a traditional computer science degree? In this first installment of our two-part series, we'll explore the origins of the bootcamp model, how programs work, who attends them, and what the data says about cost and outcomes. In Part 2, we'll look at the critiques of bootcamps and where the model may be headed.

## The Origins of Coding Bootcamps

Coding bootcamps emerged in the early 2010s in response to a clear problem: the tech industry couldn't find enough qualified developers. Demand for software engineers was skyrocketing as companies of every kind moved online and startups multiplied. Traditional universities weren't producing computer science graduates fast enough to fill the gap, and many graduates lacked the practical, job-ready skills employers wanted.

Entrepreneurs saw an opportunity. What if you could teach people the specific skills needed for an entry-level developer job in a fraction of the time a degree requires?

**Dev Bootcamp**, founded in 2012 in San Francisco, is widely credited as one of the first coding bootcamps. It offered an intensive, immersive program focused on web development, and it quickly attracted attention for its ambitious approach and strong placement results. Around the same time, other pioneers such as **App Academy**, **Hack Reactor**, **General Assembly**, and **The Flatiron School** launched their own programs.

Many of these early bootcamps experimented with business models tied directly to job placement. App Academy, for example, famously charged no upfront tuition. Instead, students paid a percentage of their first-year salary only after landing a job. Other bootcamps earned referral fees from employers who hired their graduates. These models aligned the schools' incentives with student success and helped build credibility for a new and unproven form of education.

## How Coding Bootcamps Work

### Formats and Delivery Modes

Bootcamps come in several formats designed to fit different schedules and learning preferences:

- **Full-time immersive programs** typically run 12 to 16 weeks, with students committing 40 to 60 hours or more per week.
- **Part-time programs** stretch over six months to a year, allowing students to keep their current jobs while learning in the evenings and on weekends.
- **In-person programs** take place on physical campuses, often in major tech hubs.
- **Online programs**, which have grown dramatically in recent years, offer live or self-paced instruction from anywhere.

### Subject Coverage

While early bootcamps focused almost exclusively on web development, today's programs cover a broad range of subjects, including:

- Full-stack web development
- Front-end and back-end development
- Data science and analytics
- Cybersecurity
- UX/UI design
- Mobile app development
- Product management

Bootcamps emphasize practical, project-based learning. Students typically build a portfolio of real applications that they can show to potential employers, and many programs include career services such as resume reviews, mock interviews, and networking events.

### University Partnerships

As bootcamps have matured, many universities have partnered with bootcamp providers to offer programs under their own brands. These partnerships allow universities to reach new audiences with practical, career-focused training, while bootcamp companies gain the credibility of an established institution. Students may earn a certificate from the university upon completion, though these programs generally don't carry academic credit.

## Who Attends Coding Bootcamps?

Bootcamp students come from a wide variety of backgrounds, but one group stands out: **career changers**.

Many bootcamp students already hold a bachelor's degree, often in fields unrelated to technology such as the humanities, social sciences, or business. For some, their degree didn't lead to the job or salary they hoped for. Others have spent years in careers that no longer fulfill them. Bootcamps offer these students a relatively fast and focused way to pivot into a growing, well-compensated field without returning to school for another four years.

Bootcamps also attract recent graduates looking to supplement their education with practical skills, professionals seeking to upskill in their current roles, and people without college degrees who want an accessible entry point into tech.

## Bootcamps vs. Computer Science Degrees

How do bootcamps stack up against a traditional four-year computer science degree? Published industry research offers some useful comparisons.

### Cost

According to industry research from Course Report, the average full-time coding bootcamp costs roughly $13,000 to $14,000, with prices ranging from a few thousand dollars to over $20,000. By comparison, a four-year degree can cost anywhere from tens of thousands of dollars at a public in-state university to well over $200,000 at a private institution, before factoring in living expenses and lost income.

### Duration

Most full-time bootcamps last about three to four months. A computer science degree typically takes four years. For career changers who need to keep earning, that difference is significant.

### Salary Outcomes

Research from Course Report has found that bootcamp graduates reported an average starting salary of around $65,000 to $70,000, with an average salary increase of roughly 50% compared with their pre-bootcamp jobs. Computer science graduates generally earn higher starting salaries, often in the range of $70,000 to $80,000 or more depending on the source and region, and may have access to a wider range of roles, particularly in specialized or research-heavy areas.

The comparison isn't perfectly apples to apples. Bootcamp students often already hold degrees and may bring professional experience from other fields, while CS graduates are frequently entering the workforce for the first time.

### Depth of Learning

It's important to acknowledge that bootcamps offer less depth than a traditional computer science education. A CS degree covers foundational topics like algorithms, data structures, computer architecture, operating systems, and mathematical theory in far greater detail. Those fundamentals can matter for certain roles and for long-term career growth. Bootcamps, by design, focus on the practical skills needed to get hired and be productive quickly, and graduates often need to continue learning on the job to fill in theoretical gaps.

## Looking Ahead

Coding bootcamps have carved out a meaningful place in the education landscape. They offer a faster, more affordable, and highly practical route into tech, particularly for career changers looking for a fresh start. For many students, they've been a life-changing investment.

But the model isn't without its challenges. Some of the earliest pioneers, including Dev Bootcamp itself, have since closed their doors, and questions about outcomes reporting, quality, and value have followed the industry.
