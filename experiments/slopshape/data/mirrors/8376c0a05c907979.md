# Enhanced Due Diligence Isn't the Exception. It's Part of the Job.

For many compliance teams, enhanced due diligence (EDD) is treated like an emergency procedure: something reserved for the rare, obviously suspicious customer who sets off alarms. That mindset is outdated and risky. In today's regulatory environment, EDD is a routine, predictable obligation that financial institutions must apply whenever a customer's risk profile calls for it.

If your team already runs customer due diligence (CDD), you have the foundation in place. The question isn't whether you'll need EDD, but whether you know exactly when to step up scrutiny, and whether you can do it consistently, efficiently, and in a way regulators will accept.

## The KYC Regulatory Backdrop

Know Your Customer (KYC) requirements in the United States are rooted in the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) and reinforced by the USA PATRIOT Act. Together, these laws require financial institutions to verify customer identities, understand the nature of customer relationships, and detect and report suspicious activity.

The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) sharpened these expectations with its 2016 Customer Due Diligence Final Rule, which effectively established four core elements of CDD:

1. Identifying and verifying the identity of customers
2. Identifying and verifying the identity of beneficial owners of legal entity customers
3. Understanding the nature and purpose of customer relationships to develop a customer risk profile
4. Conducting ongoing monitoring to identify and report suspicious transactions and, on a risk basis, maintain and update customer information

EDD builds on this baseline. When a customer presents higher risk, institutions are expected to gather more information, verify it more rigorously, and monitor the relationship more closely. The Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council (FFIEC) BSA/AML Examination Manual provides a practical framework for identifying that higher risk, organizing it into three categories: customers and entities, geographic locations, and products and services.

## Customers and Entities

Certain customer types carry inherently greater risk of money laundering, corruption, or terrorist financing.

### Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs)

PEPs, individuals who hold or have held prominent public positions, along with their family members and close associates, pose elevated risk because of their potential access to public funds and influence. Senior foreign political figures are specifically addressed in U.S. regulations, particularly in the context of private banking accounts. EDD for PEPs typically includes verifying source of wealth and source of funds, obtaining senior management approval, and conducting more frequent reviews.

### Beneficial Ownership and Complex Structures

Shell companies, trusts, and layered corporate structures can obscure who actually controls an account. Under FinCEN's CDD Rule, institutions must identify individuals who own 25% or more of a legal entity and at least one individual with significant control. When ownership is opaque, spans multiple jurisdictions, or includes nominee shareholders, EDD is warranted to establish who truly benefits.

### Cash-Intensive Businesses

Businesses that handle large volumes of cash, such as convenience stores, restaurants, car washes, parking garages, and money services businesses, can be used to commingle illicit funds with legitimate revenue. EDD here focuses on understanding expected transaction patterns and validating that activity aligns with the business's stated operations.

## Geographic Locations

Where a customer lives, operates, or transacts matters.

### FATF-Monitored Jurisdictions

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) publicly identifies jurisdictions with strategic AML deficiencies, including those subject to a call for action (the "blacklist") and those under increased monitoring (the "grey list"). Relationships and transactions involving these jurisdictions require heightened scrutiny, and FinCEN regularly issues advisories reinforcing FATF's findings.

### Sanctioned Countries and Parties

The Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) administers U.S. sanctions programs targeting countries, entities, and individuals. Sanctions screening is a strict-liability obligation: transacting with a sanctioned party can result in significant penalties regardless of intent. Customers with ties to comprehensively sanctioned regions, or with connections to parties on the Specially Designated Nationals (SDN) list, demand immediate and thorough review.

### High-Risk Financial Centers

Jurisdictions known for secrecy, weak regulatory oversight, or high levels of corruption and narcotics trafficking, including designated High Intensity Financial Crime Areas (HIFCAs) domestically, also warrant enhanced attention.

## Products and Services

Some offerings are more easily exploited for illicit purposes.

### Correspondent Banking

Correspondent accounts allow foreign banks to access the U.S. financial system, often on behalf of their own customers, whom the U.S. institution never sees. Section 312 of the USA PATRIOT Act requires EDD for correspondent accounts maintained for certain foreign banks, including assessing the foreign institution's AML controls, ownership, and the jurisdictions in which it operates.

### Other Higher-Risk Offerings

Private banking, wire transfers, trade finance, remote deposit capture, prepaid access, and virtual asset services can all enable rapid, cross-border, or anonymous movement of funds. When customers use these products, especially in combination with other risk factors, EDD should follow.

## What Regulators Expect

Examiners don't expect institutions to treat every customer as high risk. They expect a **risk-based approach**: a documented, defensible methodology for assessing customer risk and applying proportionate controls.

In practice, that means:

- **Clear risk-rating criteria.** Your program should define which factors elevate a customer to high risk and trigger EDD.
- **Thorough documentation.** Every EDD decision, including the information gathered, the analysis performed, and the approvals obtained, should be recorded. If it isn't documented, examiners will assume it didn't happen.
- **Ongoing monitoring.** EDD isn't a one-time event at onboarding. High-risk customers should be reviewed on a regular schedule and whenever their activity or circumstances change, such as a new PEP designation, a change in ownership, or an updated sanctions list.
- **Consistency.** Similar customers should be treated similarly, and your process should produce repeatable results.

## Automation Makes Routine EDD Practical

If EDD is a routine obligation, it needs routine infrastructure. Manual reviews struggle to keep pace with constantly updated sanctions lists, PEP databases, and FATF designations, and they introduce inconsistency and human error.

Automated identity verification and AML watchlist screening give compliance teams the ability to screen customers against global sanctions, PEP, and adverse media sources in real time, flag high-risk triggers the moment they appear, and continuously monitor existing customers as lists change. Just as importantly, automated tools create a clear audit trail that supports the documentation regulators expect.

Enhanced due diligence is not a rare escalation. It's a core, recurring part of a sound compliance program. The institutions that treat it that way, and equip their teams with the right tools, are the ones best positioned to satisfy regulators and keep financial crime out of their systems.
