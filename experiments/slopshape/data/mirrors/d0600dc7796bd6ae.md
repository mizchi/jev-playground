# ABLE Accounts vs. Special Needs Trusts: A Comparison for Fiduciaries

Since the ABLE Act became law in 2014, ABLE accounts have offered individuals with disabilities a new way to save without jeopardizing eligibility for means-tested benefits like Supplemental Security Income (SSI) and Medicaid. Yet adoption remains strikingly low. Of the millions of Americans estimated to be eligible, only a small fraction have opened accounts. For fiduciaries, including trustees, financial advisors, and attorneys, that gap represents both a missed opportunity and a call to understand how ABLE accounts compare with the more familiar Special Needs Trust (SNT).

## Eligibility and Age of Disability Onset

**ABLE accounts** are available only to individuals whose disability began before a specified age. Currently, the onset of disability must have occurred before age 26. Beginning January 1, 2026, under the ABLE Age Adjustment Act, that threshold rises to age 46, expanding eligibility to millions of additional people, including many veterans and individuals with later-onset conditions. The beneficiary must also be entitled to SSI or SSDI based on disability, or meet the disability criteria through a certification.

**Special Needs Trusts** have no age-of-onset requirement. A first-party SNT generally must be established for a beneficiary under age 65, while third-party SNTs, funded with assets belonging to someone other than the beneficiary, can be created at any age.

## Beneficiary Control and Independence

One of the most significant differences between these tools is control.

With an **ABLE account**, the beneficiary can often manage the account directly, making contributions, choosing investments, and directing distributions. This can foster independence, financial literacy, and dignity. When the beneficiary cannot manage the account, an authorized legal representative may do so.

A **Special Needs Trust** is managed by a trustee who has discretion over distributions. The beneficiary has no direct access to trust funds. This structure offers protection for beneficiaries who may be vulnerable to exploitation or who lack the capacity to manage money, but it limits autonomy.

## Contribution and Balance Limits

**ABLE accounts** are subject to an annual contribution limit tied to the federal gift tax exclusion. Employed beneficiaries who do not participate in an employer retirement plan may contribute additional earnings under the ABLE to Work provision. Total account balances are capped according to each state's 529 plan limits. For SSI purposes, only the first $100,000 in an ABLE account is disregarded; balances above that amount may cause SSI benefits to be suspended.

**Special Needs Trusts** have no annual contribution limits and no maximum balance. This makes them well suited for larger sums, such as inheritances, personal injury settlements, or significant family gifts.

## Housing Costs and SSI

Housing is an area where ABLE accounts offer a clear advantage.

Distributions from an **ABLE account** for housing expenses, including rent, mortgage payments, property taxes, and utilities, are considered qualified disability expenses and do not reduce SSI benefits.

When a **Special Needs Trust** pays for shelter costs directly, the payment is generally treated as in-kind support and maintenance, which can reduce the beneficiary's monthly SSI benefit. Trustees must weigh whether the value of the housing payment outweighs the benefit reduction.

## Tax Treatment and Funding Assets

**ABLE accounts** grow tax-free, and distributions used for qualified disability expenses are not subject to federal income tax. Some states also offer state income tax deductions for contributions. However, ABLE accounts can only accept cash contributions.

**Special Needs Trusts** can hold a wide range of assets, including real estate, securities, life insurance proceeds, and business interests. Tax treatment depends on how the trust is structured. Trust income may be taxed at compressed trust tax rates unless it qualifies for grantor trust treatment or a qualified disability trust exemption.

Both ABLE accounts and first-party SNTs are generally subject to Medicaid payback provisions upon the beneficiary's death, though states vary in how they enforce ABLE payback. Third-party SNTs are not subject to Medicaid payback.

## Working Together

ABLE accounts and Special Needs Trusts are not an either-or proposition. In many cases, they work best together. An SNT can hold larger or non-cash assets and periodically fund an ABLE account, allowing the beneficiary to manage day-to-day expenses, including housing, with greater independence and without reducing SSI.

As the 2026 age expansion approaches, fiduciaries have an important opportunity to help close the enrollment gap. Because the rules governing these tools are complex and vary by state, individuals and families should consult a qualified special needs planning attorney to determine the right strategy for their circumstances.
