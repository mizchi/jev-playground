# Why Forking Still Matters in the Enterprise

When teams move from centralized version control to Git, they tend to settle quickly on a familiar pattern: one central repository, a stable main branch, and short-lived feature branches for every piece of work. It's a great workflow. We use it extensively at Atlassian, and we recommend it to most teams getting started with DVCS.

But there's a common assumption that comes along with it, and it's one we want to challenge: that forks are an open source thing. That they exist to let strangers on the internet contribute to projects they don't have commit access to, and that inside a company, where everyone's on the same team, they're unnecessary overhead.

We think that's wrong. Forking is one of the most useful tools available to enterprise teams, and it solves problems that branches alone simply can't.

## First, What Is a Fork?

It's worth being precise, because the terminology gets muddled.

A **clone** is a local copy of a repository on a developer's machine. Every Git user makes clones; that's how you work.

A **fork** is a server-side copy of a repository. It lives alongside the original on the Git server, has its own permissions, its own branches, and its own URL. It retains a relationship with the repository it came from, so changes can flow back upstream via pull requests, and upstream changes can be synced into the fork.

That distinction matters. A clone is personal and private by nature. A fork is a shared, managed, server-side object that other people can see, review, and collaborate on, with permissions that are independent of the original.

In open source, forks are the dominant model. On public hosting platforms, the vast majority of contributions arrive as pull requests from forks, because maintainers can't hand out write access to everyone who wants to fix a typo. Forks let anyone contribute while keeping control firmly in the hands of the project's owners.

It turns out that "anyone can contribute, but owners keep control" is a problem enterprises have too.

## 1. Protect Core Shared Components While Inviting Contributions

Every large organization has code that many teams depend on: a shared UI library, an authentication service, a build toolchain, a common data model. These components are usually owned by a specific team, and that team needs to protect them. A careless change can break dozens of downstream products.

The naive solution is to lock the repository down so only the owning team can push. But that creates a bottleneck. Other teams find bugs and want features, and if the only way to get a change in is to file a ticket and wait, they'll either wait forever or start copying the code into their own projects.

Forks solve this cleanly. The owning team keeps write access to the canonical repository to themselves. Anyone else in the company can fork it, make their change, and open a pull request. The owners review, discuss, and merge on their own terms. Contribution is open; control is preserved.

This is exactly how we manage shared libraries at Atlassian. Our platform components are consumed by JIRA, Confluence, Stash, and more. Product developers regularly contribute fixes and improvements back to those components through forks, without anyone needing to grant them broad write access to critical code.

## 2. Coordinate Departments That Maintain Their Own Variants

Sometimes different parts of an organization genuinely need different versions of the same codebase. A regional division might need localization or regulatory changes. A hardware team might maintain a variant tuned for a specific device. A professional services group might carry customizations for key clients.

Branches are a poor fit for this. Long-lived variant branches in a central repository clutter it, confuse everyone else, and blur the lines of ownership. Who's allowed to push to `emea-release`? Who's responsible for keeping it up to date?

A fork makes the relationship explicit. The department owns its fork outright, with its own permissions and its own branches. It can pull improvements from upstream whenever it's ready, and contribute general-purpose changes back via pull request. The central team doesn't have to care about the variant's internal details, and the department isn't held hostage by the central team's release schedule.

## 3. Keep Central Repositories Free of Branch Noise

Feature branches are wonderful, but they multiply. In an active repository with dozens of developers, it's not unusual to see hundreds of branches: work in progress, abandoned experiments, spikes, half-finished refactors, and branches nobody remembers creating.

All of that noise lives in the central repository, where everyone has to wade through it. Branch lists become unreadable. Automated builds kick off for code that was never meant to be shared. Cleaning up becomes a political exercise.

With forks, experimental and in-progress work can live where it belongs: in the developer's or team's own fork. The central repository contains only the branches that matter to everyone, such as `master`, release branches, and features actively heading toward integration. When work is ready, it arrives as a pull request. The canonical repository stays clean, and the history stays meaningful.

## 4. Control Contractor Access

Most enterprises work with contractors, agencies, and outsourced development partners. These collaborators need to contribute code, but granting them write access to your core repositories is a real risk. Even with the best intentions, a force-push to the wrong branch can do serious damage.

Forks give you a natural boundary. Contractors get read access to the upstream repository and full control of their own fork. They work there, and every change they want to deliver comes through a pull request that your own developers review before merging. You get a clear audit trail, a mandatory review step, and zero risk of external parties pushing directly to production branches.

When the engagement ends, you revoke access to the fork. No cleanup of stray branches in your central repository required.

## 5. Give Developers Private Personal Forks

Finally, there's the simplest case, and in some ways the most valuable. Developers need a place to back up work, share half-finished ideas with a colleague, or experiment without an audience.

A local clone isn't enough. It isn't backed up, and nobody else can see it. Pushing a branch to the central repository puts unfinished work on public display. A personal fork sits right in between: it's on the server, safe and shareable, but it's yours. You decide who sees it and when.

At Atlassian, many of our developers keep personal forks of the repositories they work on most. They push freely, try risky ideas, and invite a teammate to take a look before anything touches the main repository. It encourages exactly the kind of experimentation we want, without the mess.

## Forks and Branches Are Complementary

None of this means you should abandon feature branches. The central-repository, branch-per-feature workflow is still a fantastic default. Forks are complementary: they add boundaries of ownership and permission that branches were never designed to provide.

Use branches to organize work. Use forks to organize people, teams, and trust.
