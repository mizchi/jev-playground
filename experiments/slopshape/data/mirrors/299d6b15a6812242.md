# 8 Real Upselling Examples from Brands That Get It Right (and What You Can Learn from Them)

I'll be honest: I've been upsold more times than I can count. Sometimes I didn't even notice it happening. Other times I noticed, appreciated it, and happily paid more because the upgrade genuinely made my life better. And yes, a few times I walked away feeling like I'd been talked into something I didn't need.

That range of experiences is exactly why I find upselling so fascinating. Done well, it's one of the most customer-friendly ways to grow revenue. Done poorly, it's a fast track to buyer's remorse and churn.

In this post, I'll walk through eight real upselling examples from brands you'll recognize across SaaS, ecommerce, travel rewards, and subscriptions. For each one, I'll break down the tactic, why it works, and what you can borrow for your own business. And at the end, I'll share how contact scoring can help you figure out *who* is actually ready for an upsell, so you're not guessing.

Let's dig in.

## What Is Upselling?

Upselling is the practice of encouraging a customer to buy a more expensive version of a product, an upgraded plan, or an add-on that increases the value of their purchase.

It's different from cross-selling, which is about recommending *related* products (think: "You bought a camera, want a memory card?"). Upselling is about moving up: the bigger plan, the premium tier, the deluxe package.

### Why Upselling Matters

Here's the simple math: it's usually far easier and cheaper to sell to someone who already knows and trusts you than to win over a brand-new customer. Your existing customers have already cleared the hardest hurdle. They've decided you're worth paying.

Upselling helps you:

- **Increase average order value and customer lifetime value.** Each customer becomes more valuable over time.
- **Grow revenue without proportionally increasing acquisition costs.** You're not paying to find these people again.
- **Improve retention.** When customers upgrade to a plan that fits their needs better, they tend to get more value, and customers who get more value tend to stick around.

But (and this is a big but) the upsell has to genuinely serve the customer. I'll come back to that point a few times, because it's the difference between upselling that builds loyalty and upselling that erodes it.

## 1. Highlighted Pricing Tiers: HubSpot

If you've ever visited a SaaS pricing page, you've almost certainly seen this one. HubSpot's pricing pages lay out multiple tiers side by side and visually highlight one of them, typically with a "Most Popular" label or a contrasting color.

The first time I compared marketing tools, I remember my eye going straight to the highlighted plan. I didn't consciously decide to look there. The design did it for me.

**Why it works:** Highlighting a tier does two things. It reduces decision fatigue by offering a suggested "default" choice, and it uses social proof: if most people pick this plan, it's probably the safe bet. It also anchors the options. When the highlighted middle tier sits next to a much pricier enterprise option, the middle suddenly looks very reasonable.

**What to borrow:** Highlight the plan that genuinely fits most of your customers, not simply the most expensive one. If the highlighted plan leaves people feeling overcharged, you've traded short-term revenue for long-term frustration.

## 2. Defaulting to the Premium Option: Annual Plans and Pre-Selected Upgrades

Plenty of subscription and SaaS companies, from Grammarly to Canva, set up their checkout or pricing toggles so the premium or annual option is pre-selected. When you land on the page, the upgraded choice is already the default. You have to actively switch to the cheaper option.

I've caught myself doing this with software subscriptions: the annual plan was toggled on, the savings were displayed right there, and I just... went with it. In most cases, I was glad I did.

**Why it works:** Defaults are powerful. People tend to stick with whatever is pre-selected because changing it requires effort and a conscious decision. When the default is also framed as a better deal (like "Save 20% with annual billing"), it feels like the smart choice rather than a sales push.

**What to borrow:** Defaults should be defensible. Pre-select the option that delivers the best value for the typical customer, and make switching easy and obvious. Hiding the cheaper option or making it hard to find crosses the line from persuasion into manipulation, and customers notice.

## 3. In-App Upgrade Prompts: Spotify

Spotify is a master of the in-app upsell. Free users hit natural friction points: ads between songs, limited skips, no offline listening. At each of those moments, Spotify gently reminds them that Premium removes the limitation.

I held out on the free plan for a while. Then one day I was on a long flight, realized I couldn't download my playlists, and upgraded right there in the app. Spotify didn't need to convince me in that moment. The product had already done the convincing.

**Why it works:** In-app prompts meet users exactly when they feel the pain the upgrade solves. There's no need for a lengthy sales pitch because the value is immediate and obvious. The context does the selling.

**What to borrow:** Map out the moments in your product where users bump into limits. That's where a well-timed, helpful prompt belongs. Keep it friendly and easy to dismiss. If users feel nagged or blocked at every turn, the prompts start to feel punitive instead of helpful.

## 4. Custom Enterprise Plans: Slack

Slack offers standard self-serve plans, but at the top of its pricing page, you'll find an enterprise option with a "Contact Sales" call to action instead of a fixed price. That tier includes advanced security, compliance features, and support designed for large organizations.

**Why it works:** Large companies have complex needs, such as security reviews, data residency requirements, and custom integrations, that don't fit neatly into a standard plan. A custom enterprise plan signals, "We can handle your scale." It also opens the door to a sales conversation where the team can tailor the offer and pricing to the customer's actual requirements.

From the buyer's side, I've been on those calls. What made the good ones good was that the sales rep asked about our needs first and pitched second. The bad ones felt like a script.

**What to borrow:** If you serve customers of widely different sizes, consider a custom top tier. Use the sales conversation to learn about the customer's challenges, not just to close. The more your enterprise offer reflects what they actually need, the easier the upgrade becomes.

## 5. Discounted Upgrades for Past Buyers: Apple Trade In

Apple's trade-in program lets existing customers exchange an older device for credit toward a new one. If you've got an aging iPhone, Apple makes it easy to put its value toward the latest model.

The last time I upgraded my phone, the trade-in credit was the deciding factor. I wasn't planning to buy that year, but seeing the lower effective price made the upgrade feel like a reasonable step rather than a splurge.

**Why it works:** Past buyers already trust the brand and are familiar with the product. Offering them a discount or credit toward an upgrade rewards their loyalty and lowers the barrier to spending more. It also keeps them in the ecosystem instead of drifting to a competitor.

**What to borrow:** Look at your past customers and consider what a loyalty-based upgrade offer might look like: a discount on the next tier, credit for their current plan, or an exclusive upgrade price. Make them feel recognized for coming back.

## 6. Recommendation Feeds: Amazon

Amazon's recommendation engine is legendary. As you browse, you'll see sections like "Customers also bought," "Compare with similar items," and suggestions for higher-end versions of what you're looking at. Often, you'll notice a premium alternative with better reviews or more features sitting just a few dollars away.

I once went looking for a basic pair of headphones and ended up with a noticeably better pair after the product comparison showed me what an extra small amount would get me. I didn't feel pushed. I felt informed.

**Why it works:** Recommendation feeds use browsing and purchase data to surface relevant upgrades. Because the suggestions are personalized and presented as helpful comparisons, they feel less like an upsell and more like shopping advice.

**What to borrow:** You don't need Amazon's data science team to do this. Even simple logic, like showing a premium version alongside a standard product or displaying a comparison table, can nudge customers toward better options. The key is relevance: recommend upgrades that actually make sense for what the customer is trying to do.

## 7. Well-Timed Email Offers: Airline and Hotel Upgrade Emails

Travel brands are fantastic at the well-timed upsell email. After booking a flight, many airlines send emails in the days leading up to departure offering seat upgrades, extra legroom, or the chance to bid on a premium cabin seat. Hotel loyalty programs like Marriott Bonvoy do something similar, offering room upgrades or add-ons before your stay.

I'll admit it: an upgrade email arriving the night before a long-haul flight is very persuasive. I've accepted more than one of those offers because the timing was perfect. I was already thinking about the trip, and the upgrade suddenly felt very real and worthwhile.

**Why it works:** Timing is everything. The customer has already committed to the purchase, they're anticipating the experience, and the offer arrives right when the value of an upgrade is top of mind. It feels like an opportunity, not a pitch.

**What to borrow:** Think about the moments in your customer journey when an upgrade feels most relevant: right after onboarding, when a customer hits a usage milestone, before a renewal, or just before a key event. Use email automation to send offers at those moments rather than blasting everyone at once.

## 8. VIP Memberships: Amazon Prime and Sephora Beauty Insider

VIP memberships turn upselling into an ongoing relationship. Amazon Prime bundles fast shipping, streaming, and exclusive deals into a single membership. Sephora's Beauty Insider program rewards customers with tiers that unlock perks, such as birthday gifts, exclusive events, and bigger rewards, as they spend more.

I've been a member of both kinds of programs, and I'll say this: the perks genuinely change how I shop. Once I had Prime, I stopped comparing shipping costs elsewhere. With tiered rewards programs, I've definitely felt the pull to reach the next tier.

**Why it works:** Memberships create a sense of belonging and exclusivity. They also shift the customer's mindset. Once they've paid for (or earned) VIP status, they want to get the most out of it, which drives repeat purchases and deeper engagement.

**What to borrow:** Consider whether a membership or tiered loyalty program fits your business. The perks don't have to be elaborate. Early access, exclusive content, priority support, or member-only pricing can all create meaningful value. Just make sure the benefits are real and easy to understand.

## A Word of Caution: Don't Push Customers Beyond Their Budget

I've shared a lot of examples where upselling worked well for me as a buyer. But I've also had the opposite experience: being pressured into a plan I didn't need, getting stuck with features I never used, and eventually canceling out of frustration.

Here's what I've learned from both sides of the table:

- **Upsell to fit, not to maximize.** The best upsell matches the customer's real needs. If they don't need the enterprise plan, don't push it.
- **Respect budgets.** A customer stretched beyond what they can comfortably afford is a customer at risk of churning, requesting refunds, or leaving a negative review.
- **Make downgrading and saying "no" easy.** Paradoxically, customers trust you more when they know they're not trapped.
- **Be transparent.** Clear pricing and honest comparisons build credibility. Hidden fees and confusing terms destroy it.

The goal isn't to squeeze every possible dollar out of a single transaction. It's to build a relationship where customers happily grow with you over time.

## How Contact Scoring Helps You Upsell at the Right Moment

Every one of these examples has something in common: the upsell works because it reaches the right person at the right time. Spotify's prompt lands when a user hits a limit. The airline email arrives right before the flight. Apple's trade-in offer targets people with an older device.

For most sales and marketing teams, figuring out who's ready for an upsell is the hardest part. You've got a database full of customers, but which ones are primed for an upgrade, and which ones would be annoyed by the pitch?

That's where contact scoring comes in.

Contact scoring assigns a score to each contact based on two key factors:

- **Fit:** How closely does this contact match your ideal customer profile for the upgrade? Think company size, industry, role, or current plan.
- **Activity:** How is the contact engaging with you? Are they logging in more often, hitting usage limits, visiting your pricing page, opening upgrade emails, or attending webinars about advanced features?

When you combine fit and activity, you get a clear picture of which customers are most likely to be ready for an upsell. Instead of sending the same upgrade email to everyone, your team can focus on the contacts who are showing real buying signals, and reach out at exactly the moment the offer is most relevant.
