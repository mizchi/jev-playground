## What Is an EKG/ECG?

An electrocardiogram is a test that records the electrical activity of the heart. The terms EKG and ECG mean the same thing. "ECG" comes from the English spelling, *electrocardiogram*, while "EKG" comes from the German *Elektrokardiogramm*. In the United States, both are used interchangeably, and many clinicians prefer "EKG" to avoid confusion with "EEG," a test of brain activity.

### How an EKG Works

Every heartbeat is triggered by an electrical signal. That signal starts in a small cluster of cells in the upper right chamber of the heart called the sinoatrial (SA) node, often described as the heart's natural pacemaker. The impulse travels through the upper chambers (the atria), causing them to contract, then passes through the atrioventricular (AV) node and down into the lower chambers (the ventricles), which contract and pump blood to the lungs and the rest of the body.

An EKG machine detects these tiny electrical signals through electrodes placed on the skin of the chest, arms, and legs. A standard resting EKG uses 10 electrodes to produce 12 "leads." Each lead represents a different view of the heart's electrical activity from a particular angle.

The machine amplifies the signals and records them as a waveform, printed on paper or displayed on a screen. Each part of the waveform corresponds to a specific event in the cardiac cycle:

- **P wave:** The electrical activation of the atria.
- **QRS complex:** The rapid activation of the ventricles.
- **T wave:** The ventricles recovering, or "resetting," before the next beat.

By analyzing the shape, size, timing, and rhythm of these waves, clinicians can learn a great deal about how the heart is functioning. The test is painless, takes only a few minutes, and involves no radiation.

### What Conditions Can an EKG Help Diagnose?

EKGs are used to detect and monitor a wide range of heart conditions, including:

- **Arrhythmias:** Irregular heart rhythms, such as atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or heart rates that are too fast (tachycardia) or too slow (bradycardia).
- **Heart attacks:** Both current heart attacks (myocardial infarctions) and evidence of past ones.
- **Coronary artery disease:** Signs of reduced blood flow to the heart muscle, known as ischemia.
- **Conduction abnormalities:** Problems with how electrical signals travel through the heart, such as bundle branch blocks or heart block.
- **Enlarged heart chambers:** Thickened heart walls or enlarged chambers, which may point to high blood pressure or other conditions.
- **Electrolyte imbalances:** Abnormal levels of potassium, calcium, or magnesium can change the EKG pattern.
- **Effects of medications:** Some drugs can affect the heart's electrical activity, and EKGs help clinicians monitor for these effects.
- **Pacemaker function:** Confirming that an implanted device is working properly.

### Where Are EKG Machines Used?

Because they're fast, portable, and highly informative, EKG machines are found in nearly every corner of healthcare:

- **Emergency departments:** For rapid evaluation of chest pain, shortness of breath, and other urgent symptoms.
- **Hospitals:** In inpatient units, intensive care units, cardiac care units, and preoperative assessment areas.
- **Cardiology practices:** For diagnosis, ongoing monitoring, and stress testing.
- **Primary care clinics:** As part of routine checkups and screenings.
- **Urgent care centers:** To help triage patients with cardiac symptoms.
- **Ambulatory surgery centers:** For preoperative screening.
- **Occupational health and sports medicine:** For pre-employment physicals and athlete screenings.

For facilities that perform a high volume of EKGs, choosing a reliable, feature-rich machine is essential.

## Refurbished vs. Used: What's the Difference?

When you're shopping for pre-owned medical equipment, you'll often see the terms "refurbished" and "used." They might sound similar, but there's an important difference.

**Used equipment** is typically sold as is. It may have been removed from service at another facility and resold without testing, repairs, or cleaning. You may not know the device's service history, whether it's fully functional, or whether it meets manufacturer specifications. Buying used can mean hidden problems and unexpected repair costs down the road.

**Refurbished equipment** has been thoroughly inspected, tested, repaired, and restored to meet the original equipment manufacturer's (OEM) specifications. It's cleaned, calibrated, and verified to perform as intended. In short, refurbished equipment is ready for clinical use.
