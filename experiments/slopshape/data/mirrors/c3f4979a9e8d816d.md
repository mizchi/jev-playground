# Leaked Credentials: Why They Matter and How Castle Is Helping You Protect Your Users

Every week seems to bring news of another data breach. A retailer, a social network, a gaming platform, a SaaS tool. Millions of email addresses and passwords spill out into the world, and most people shrug and move on. After all, if it wasn't your bank that got breached, why worry?

The answer comes down to one very human habit: password reuse. And it's exactly why leaked credentials databases have become one of the most powerful tools in an attacker's arsenal.

## What Is a Leaked Credentials Database?

A leaked credentials database is a collection of usernames, email addresses, and passwords that have been exposed in data breaches. Sometimes the passwords are in plain text. Sometimes they're hashed, but weakly enough that attackers can crack them quickly. Either way, once the data is out, it doesn't stay in one place for long.

The journey typically looks something like this:

1. **The breach.** An attacker compromises a company's systems and exfiltrates user data.
2. **The dark web.** The stolen data is often sold privately on underground marketplaces or shared in closed forums, where it commands a premium while it's fresh.
3. **The spread.** Over time, the data changes hands again and again. It gets bundled with other breaches into massive "combo lists" containing hundreds of millions, or even billions, of credential pairs.
4. **The public forums.** Eventually, much of this data ends up freely available on hacking forums, file-sharing sites, and messaging channels, where anyone with a little curiosity can download it.

By that point, the credentials have been used, reused, and weaponized many times over.

## Why Password Reuse Makes Leaks So Dangerous

If everyone used a unique password for every site, a breach at one company would mostly stay contained to that company. But that's not how people behave. Studies consistently show that a large majority of users reuse passwords across multiple accounts, often with only minor variations.

That's what makes credential stuffing so effective. Attackers take leaked email and password combinations and use automated tools to try them against login pages across the internet. Even if only a small percentage of attempts succeed, the sheer scale of these lists means attackers can compromise thousands of accounts in a single campaign.

The account that gets taken over might not be the one that was breached. It could be yours. A user whose credentials leaked from a forum they signed up for a decade ago might have reused that same password on your platform. To your login system, the attacker looks exactly like the legitimate user.

## The White Hats Fighting Back

Thankfully, the security community hasn't stood still. Troy Hunt's **Have I Been Pwned** has become the go-to resource for individuals who want to check whether their email address has appeared in a known breach. Its Pwned Passwords service lets developers check whether a password has been exposed, without ever revealing the password itself.

Password managers like **1Password** have built breach monitoring directly into their products through features like Watchtower, alerting users when their credentials show up in a known leak and nudging them to update weak or reused passwords.

These efforts have done enormous good. But they share a common limitation: they depend on users taking action. Most people never visit Have I Been Pwned. Many don't use a password manager at all. The reality is that only a small fraction of users proactively monitor their exposure.

## Your Platform Is the Guardian

That's where customer-facing websites and applications come in. When a user creates an account on your platform, they're trusting you to keep it safe. Whether or not it's fair, they'll hold you responsible if an attacker gets in, even if the password was leaked somewhere else entirely.

This makes you a guardian of your users' security. You're in a unique position to spot risk that users can't or won't see for themselves, and to step in before an account is compromised. Knowing which of your users have credentials floating around in leaked databases is a crucial piece of that puzzle.

## Introducing Castle's Leaked Credentials Database

We're excited to share that Castle now maintains its own aggregated database of leaked credentials, compiled from a wide range of known breaches and public dumps. This data is now working behind the scenes to help protect your users.

### Smarter Risk Scoring

Breach exposure is now a signal in Castle's risk scoring. When a user logs in, Castle considers whether their credentials have appeared in known leaks alongside the other behavioral and device signals we already analyze. A login from an unfamiliar device, using credentials known to be exposed, is a very different situation from a routine login on a trusted laptop, and Castle's risk assessment reflects that.

This means more accurate decisions about when to challenge a user with step-up authentication, when to block a suspicious attempt, and when to let a legitimate user through without friction.

### New Dashboard Features

We've also added new features to the Castle dashboard to give your team visibility into breach exposure across your user base:

- **Exposure flags.** Users whose credentials have appeared in leaked databases are clearly flagged in their user profiles.
- **Filtering.** You can filter your user list to see exactly which users are exposed, making it easy to prioritize outreach or investigation.
- **Context at a glance.** When reviewing suspicious activity, your team can immediately see whether breach exposure may be a factor.

With this information, you can take proactive steps like prompting exposed users to reset their passwords, encouraging them to enable multi-factor authentication, or monitoring their accounts more closely.
