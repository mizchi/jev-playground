# Closing the Gaps: How PBMs, Pharmacies, and Providers Can Tackle Medication Nonadherence

Medication nonadherence remains one of the most persistent and costly challenges in American healthcare. Despite decades of research, new therapies, and growing awareness, roughly half of patients with chronic conditions do not take their medications as prescribed. The consequences ripple across the entire system, affecting patient outcomes, plan costs, and quality ratings alike.

For pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs), pharmacies, and providers, the question is no longer whether nonadherence matters, but how to identify and close the gaps that drive it before they lead to avoidable harm.

## The Clinical and Financial Stakes

The scale of the problem is striking. Studies have estimated that nonadherence contributes to approximately 125,000 deaths annually in the United States and accounts for up to 10% of hospitalizations. Financially, the cost of avoidable healthcare spending tied to nonadherence has been estimated at between $100 billion and $300 billion each year.

These figures reflect real clinical deterioration. Patients who skip antihypertensives face higher risk of stroke and heart attack. Those who abandon statins lose cardiovascular protection. Diabetic patients who miss doses of oral agents or insulin see worsening glycemic control and increased risk of complications. Each missed refill represents a potential downstream event that is both preventable and expensive.

## Cost: A Leading Barrier to Adherence

Among the many factors that influence adherence, including side effects, complex regimens, health literacy, and forgetfulness, cost consistently ranks near the top. Research has shown that as patient out-of-pocket costs increase, prescription abandonment rates rise sharply. When a patient arrives at the pharmacy counter and faces a copay they cannot afford, the prescription frequently goes unfilled.

This is especially true for brand-name medications. High-deductible health plans and coinsurance structures expose patients to a larger share of list prices, and many simply walk away.

### The Pharmacist's Role in Steering Toward Generics

Pharmacists are uniquely positioned to intervene at this critical moment. When a brand-name prescription is abandoned or delayed, pharmacists can work with prescribers to recommend therapeutically equivalent or clinically appropriate lower-cost alternatives, often generics with established efficacy and safety profiles.

Evidence supports this approach. Patients initiated on generic medications demonstrate higher adherence rates than those started on brand-name equivalents, largely due to lower cost-sharing. Equipping pharmacists with real-time insight into unfilled claims and viable alternatives can turn a lost prescription into a successful therapy start.

## Accountability Is Increasing

The pressure to improve adherence is not merely clinical. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) weights medication adherence measures heavily in the Medicare Part D Star Ratings, specifically for diabetes medications, renin-angiotensin system (RAS) antagonists, and statins. These triple-weighted measures significantly affect overall plan ratings, which in turn influence bonus payments and member enrollment.

As CMS continues to refine its quality programs, including adjustments to measure methodology and greater emphasis on health equity, plans and their partners will face mounting accountability for adherence performance. Organizations that rely on reactive, retrospective approaches will find themselves at a growing disadvantage.

## Moving Beyond One-Size-Fits-All Programs

Many traditional adherence programs apply the same interventions, such as automated refill reminders or generic outreach calls, to broad populations. While these tactics have their place, they often fail to address the specific reasons individual patients stop taking their medications.

A more effective approach segments patients by risk and need. Consider the newly diagnosed patient. Research indicates that a substantial portion of patients who start a new chronic medication discontinue within the first year, with many dropping off after the first fill. These patients may not yet understand their condition, may experience early side effects, or may be uncertain about the medication's value. Targeted early outreach, including pharmacist counseling at initiation and follow-up within the first 30 days, can dramatically improve long-term persistence.

Other segments warrant distinct strategies as well: patients on complex polypharmacy regimens, those with cost-sensitive benefit designs, individuals with behavioral health conditions, and members facing social determinants of health such as transportation barriers. Precision targeting ensures resources are directed where they will have the greatest impact.

## Digital Therapeutics and Compliance Packaging

Innovation is expanding the toolkit available to address nonadherence.

**Digital therapeutics and connected tools** include smartphone apps, smart pill bottles, and remote monitoring platforms that remind patients to take doses, track behavior, and alert care teams to missed medications. Some evidence-based digital therapeutics have received FDA clearance and deliver structured behavioral interventions alongside pharmacotherapy.

**Compliance packaging**, such as blister packs and multi-dose adherence packaging organized by date and time, simplifies complex regimens, particularly for elderly patients and those managing multiple chronic conditions. Studies have shown that such packaging, combined with medication synchronization programs, can meaningfully improve proportion of days covered (PDC).

These solutions work best when deployed strategically to the patients most likely to benefit, rather than universally.

## Proactive, Population-Level Analytics: Intervening Before Nonadherence Occurs

The common thread across these strategies is timing. Too often, adherence programs respond after the gap has already appeared: after a refill has lapsed, after a PDC threshold has been missed, or after a patient has been hospitalized.

Proactive, population-level analytics change that equation. By integrating pharmacy claims, medical data, benefit design, and patient-level risk factors, advanced analytics platforms can identify which members are most likely to become nonadherent, and why, before it happens. Predictive models can flag newly diagnosed patients at risk of early discontinuation, highlight members whose rising out-of-pocket costs threaten continuity, and surface opportunities for generic substitution or benefit adjustments.

With these insights, PBMs, pharmacies, and providers can coordinate targeted interventions, allocate resources efficiently, and demonstrate measurable improvements in both outcomes and quality ratings.

Medication nonadherence is not an unsolvable problem. It is a series of identifiable gaps. With the right data and the right partners, those gaps can be closed, one patient segment at a time.
