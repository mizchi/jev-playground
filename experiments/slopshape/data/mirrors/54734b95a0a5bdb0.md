# Biologics vs. Biosimilars: What Plan Sponsors Need to Know About the Next Wave of Drug Savings

Specialty drugs account for a small share of prescriptions but a large and growing share of pharmacy spend. For many plan sponsors, a handful of high-cost biologic medications now drive a significant portion of total drug costs. At the same time, patents on some of the best-selling biologics in history are expiring, opening the door to lower-cost alternatives known as biosimilars.

Understanding the difference between biologics and biosimilars, and how they differ from traditional generics, is essential for benefits decision-makers who want to control costs without compromising patient care. Here's what you need to know.

## What Are Biologics?

Biologics are medications made from living organisms, such as cells, yeast, or bacteria. Unlike traditional drugs, which are typically small molecules created through chemical synthesis, biologics are large, complex molecules produced through biological processes. Examples include monoclonal antibodies, vaccines, insulin, and therapeutic proteins.

Because of their complexity, biologics are used to treat many serious and chronic conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's disease, psoriasis, multiple sclerosis, and many forms of cancer. They have transformed treatment for millions of patients.

That complexity also makes them expensive. Biologics require sophisticated manufacturing, careful handling, and extensive clinical development. Many cost tens of thousands of dollars per patient per year, and they represent one of the fastest-growing categories of pharmacy spend.

## What Are Biosimilars?

A biosimilar is a biologic medication that is highly similar to an existing FDA-approved biologic, known as the reference product. To earn approval, a biosimilar manufacturer must demonstrate that there are no clinically meaningful differences between the biosimilar and the reference product in terms of safety, purity, and potency.

Biosimilars are approved through an abbreviated pathway created by the Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act (BPCIA) of 2010. This pathway allows manufacturers to rely in part on the reference product's existing data, reducing development time and cost. The result is a product that works the same way as the original biologic, for the same conditions, at a lower price.

## How Biosimilars Differ From Generics

It's common to think of biosimilars as "generic biologics," but the comparison isn't quite accurate.

Generic drugs are exact chemical copies of small-molecule brand-name drugs. Because small molecules can be precisely replicated, a generic contains the identical active ingredient as its brand counterpart. Approval largely rests on demonstrating bioequivalence.

Biologics, by contrast, are produced in living systems, and even the original manufacturer's product can vary slightly from batch to batch. It is not possible to create an identical copy of a biologic. Instead, biosimilar manufacturers must show that their product is highly similar to the reference product, with only minor differences in clinically inactive components.

This distinction has practical consequences. Biosimilar development is more complex and expensive than generic development, typically costing more and taking longer. As a result, biosimilars generally offer smaller discounts than generics. Where generics can be priced 80 to 90 percent below the brand, biosimilars have historically launched at discounts ranging from roughly 15 to 35 percent, though some recent launches have come in far lower.

## What Interchangeability Means for Pharmacy Substitution

Not all biosimilars can be swapped for their reference product at the pharmacy counter. The FDA offers an additional designation called **interchangeability**.

An interchangeable biosimilar meets additional requirements showing that it can be expected to produce the same clinical result as the reference product in any given patient, and that switching between the two does not increase risk or reduce effectiveness. Once a biosimilar is designated interchangeable, a pharmacist may substitute it for the reference product without the prescriber's intervention, subject to state pharmacy laws.

For plan sponsors, interchangeability matters because it makes substitution easier and can accelerate adoption. Without it, moving members to a biosimilar typically requires a new prescription, which can slow uptake and limit savings.

## Humira: The Biosimilar Test Case

No drug illustrates the stakes better than Humira (adalimumab). For years, Humira was the best-selling drug in the world, generating roughly $20 billion in annual global revenue at its peak. It treats a range of autoimmune conditions, and its list price climbed to more than $6,000 per month.

Humira biosimilars were available in Europe years before they reached the U.S., where patent settlements delayed competition until 2023. Amgen's Amjevita launched first in January 2023, followed by a wave of additional adalimumab biosimilars in July 2023, including Cyltezo, Hyrimoz, Hadlima, Yusimry, Hulio, Idacio, and Yuflyma. Boehringer Ingelheim's Cyltezo became the first biosimilar to receive an interchangeable designation for Humira, and others have since followed.

Several of these products launched at two price points: a high list price with larger rebates, and a low list price, some more than 80 percent below Humira's list price. This pricing structure creates both opportunity and complexity for plan sponsors. Depending on formulary strategy, rebate arrangements, and PBM contracts, the net savings of moving from Humira to a biosimilar can vary significantly.

Early adoption was slow, as many PBMs initially kept Humira on formulary alongside biosimilars. That changed as major PBMs began removing Humira from their national formularies in favor of biosimilars, driving a rapid shift in market share. For plan sponsors, the Humira story is a clear lesson: savings from biosimilars are real, but capturing them depends on understanding the market and actively managing formulary decisions.

## A Growing Market

Humira is only the beginning. Biologics such as Stelara (ustekinumab) now face biosimilar competition, and other blockbuster products are approaching patent expiration in the coming years. Analysts have projected that the global biosimilars market could grow from roughly $30 billion in 2023 to well over $70 billion by the end of the decade, with some estimates exceeding $100 billion.

In the U.S., biosimilars have the potential to reduce drug spending by tens of billions of dollars over the next several years. For employers and health plans, this represents one of the most significant cost-management opportunities in the specialty pharmacy space.

## What This Means for Plan Sponsors

To make the most of biosimilars, benefits decision-makers should:

- **Monitor patent expirations** and upcoming biosimilar launches that affect drugs in their population.
- **Understand net cost**, not just list price, by accounting for rebates and pricing structures.
- **Review PBM formulary strategy** to ensure biosimilars are positioned to deliver savings.
- **Consider member experience**, including communication and support when transitioning therapies.
- **Forecast the impact** of biosimilar adoption on future drug spend.
