# The Cloud-First Challenge: 6 Problems Tech Companies Must Solve in 2023

The cloud market is booming. Businesses of every size are moving workloads, applications, and entire tech stacks to the cloud, and spending on cloud services continues to climb year after year. For engineering and business leaders, the opportunity is enormous: faster innovation, greater scalability, and the flexibility to adapt to changing markets.

But rapid growth brings real challenges. Building a cloud-first tech stack isn't as simple as flipping a switch. It requires the right people, careful planning, and a clear understanding of the risks involved.

In this article, we'll look at six of the biggest challenges companies face as they build cloud-first tech stacks in 2023, and what leaders can do to address them.

## What Is Cloud Computing?

At its core, cloud computing is the delivery of computing services, including servers, storage, databases, networking, software, and analytics, over the internet. Instead of buying and maintaining physical hardware in their own data centers, companies rent these resources from cloud providers such as Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud, typically paying only for what they use.

The appeal is straightforward. Companies can scale resources up or down on demand, launch new products faster, and reduce the burden of managing infrastructure.

## How the Pandemic Accelerated Cloud Adoption

Cloud adoption was growing steadily before 2020, but the COVID-19 pandemic dramatically accelerated it. Almost overnight, businesses had to support remote workforces, shift to digital customer experiences, and keep operations running without access to physical offices.

The cloud made that possible. Collaboration tools, virtual desktops, ecommerce platforms, and remote access solutions all relied on cloud infrastructure. Companies that had been hesitant to migrate suddenly found themselves fast-tracking plans that had been years away.

That momentum hasn't slowed. But as cloud adoption matures, the challenges of doing it well have become more visible.

## Challenge 1: The Shortage of Cloud Engineering Talent

The single biggest obstacle for many companies is finding people with the skills to build and manage cloud infrastructure. Demand for cloud engineers, architects, DevOps specialists, and cloud security professionals far exceeds supply, and competition for experienced talent is fierce.

Companies can tackle the shortage in three main ways:

**Hiring.** Expanding your search beyond traditional candidate profiles can open up new talent pools. Skills-based hiring, which focuses on what candidates can actually do rather than where they went to school or which companies they've worked for, helps teams identify capable engineers who might otherwise be overlooked. Technical assessments that reflect real-world cloud tasks make it easier to evaluate skills objectively.

**Retention.** Holding onto the cloud talent you already have is just as important as hiring new people. Competitive compensation, meaningful projects, flexible work arrangements, and clear career growth paths all help keep engineers engaged.

**Upskilling.** Many organizations already have talented developers and IT professionals who can grow into cloud roles. Investing in training, certifications, and hands-on learning opportunities helps build cloud expertise internally, and shows employees that the company is investing in their future.

The most resilient organizations combine all three approaches.

## Challenge 2: Migration Is More Than Moving Data

It's easy to think of cloud migration as simply transferring data and applications from on-premises servers to the cloud. In reality, it's far more complex.

Successful migration often requires rethinking how applications are designed. Legacy systems may need to be refactored or rebuilt to take full advantage of cloud-native features like autoscaling, microservices, and managed services. Simply "lifting and shifting" old applications can lead to poor performance and higher costs.

Migration also affects people and processes. Teams need to adopt new workflows, tools, and ways of collaborating. Governance policies, security controls, and monitoring practices must be updated. And throughout it all, the business needs to keep running with minimal disruption.

Leaders should treat migration as a strategic transformation, not just a technical project, with clear goals, phased timelines, and strong communication across teams.

## Challenge 3: Vetting Vendors for Security and Compliance

When you move to the cloud, you're entrusting sensitive data and critical systems to third-party providers. That makes vendor security and compliance vetting essential.

Cloud security typically operates under a shared responsibility model. The provider secures the underlying infrastructure, while the customer is responsible for securing their own data, applications, access controls, and configurations. Misunderstanding where those lines fall is a common source of security gaps.

Before choosing a vendor, companies should evaluate:

- Security certifications and third-party audits.
- Compliance with relevant regulations and industry standards, such as GDPR, HIPAA, or SOC 2.
- Data encryption practices, both in transit and at rest.
- Incident response procedures and breach notification policies.
- Data residency options for organizations with geographic requirements.

The same scrutiny should apply to the growing ecosystem of SaaS tools and third-party services that plug into your cloud environment.

## Challenge 4: Planning for Backup and Redundancy

Cloud providers are highly reliable, but they aren't immune to outages. Service disruptions, configuration errors, cyberattacks, and accidental deletions can all put data and operations at risk.

That's why backup and redundancy planning remains critical. Companies should consider:

- **Regular, automated backups** stored in separate locations or regions.
- **Multi-region or multi-zone deployments** to keep services available if one area goes down.
- **Disaster recovery plans** with clear recovery time and recovery point objectives.
- **Regular testing** to make sure backups and failover processes actually work when needed.

Some organizations also pursue multi-cloud or hybrid strategies to reduce dependence on a single provider, though these approaches add complexity of their own.

## Challenge 5: Rising Costs

One of the cloud's biggest selling points is cost efficiency. But without careful management, cloud spending can spiral quickly.

Common culprits include over-provisioned resources, idle instances, unused storage, data transfer fees, and a lack of visibility into who is spending what. As organizations scale, these costs can add up fast.

Many companies are turning to FinOps, a practice that brings together engineering, finance, and business teams to manage cloud spending collaboratively. Key strategies include:

- Monitoring usage and spending in real time.
- Right-sizing resources to match actual demand.
- Taking advantage of reserved instances or savings plans for predictable workloads.
- Setting budgets and alerts to catch unexpected spikes.
- Building cost awareness into engineering culture.

## Challenge 6: The Uncertain Impact of a Possible Recession

Economic uncertainty is weighing on many businesses heading into 2023. Talk of a possible recession has leaders reexamining budgets, and technology spending is no exception.

The effect on cloud adoption is hard to predict. On one hand, tighter budgets may slow or delay some migration projects, and companies may scrutinize cloud spending more closely. On the other hand, the cloud's flexibility and pay-as-you-go model can help businesses cut infrastructure costs and adapt quickly to changing conditions, which may make it even more attractive in a downturn.

For many organizations, the likely result is a shift in focus: less emphasis on rapid expansion and more on optimization, efficiency, and getting the most value from existing cloud investments.

## The Long-Term View: Cloud Adoption Is Inevitable

The challenges facing cloud-first companies in 2023 are real, from talent shortages and complex migrations to security, resilience, costs, and economic uncertainty. None of them are trivial, and each requires thoughtful planning and strong leadership.

But the long-term direction is clear. The cloud has become the foundation of modern technology, powering everything from everyday business applications to cutting-edge innovations in artificial intelligence and data analytics. Short-term headwinds may shape the pace of adoption, but they won't change its trajectory.

Companies that invest now in the right people, processes, and strategies will be best positioned to thrive in a cloud-first future.
