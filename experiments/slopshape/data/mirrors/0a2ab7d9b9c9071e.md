# Forgot a Tampon? You're Not Careless. Let's Talk Honestly About TSS.

Lately, toxic shock syndrome has been all over the news and social media. Stories about people who forgot a tampon, sometimes for days, have gone viral, and the comment sections are brutal. "How do you forget something like that?" "That's just irresponsible." "Gross."

As an OB-GYN, I want to say this clearly: forgetting a tampon is an ordinary human mistake. I see it in my practice regularly. It happens to teenagers and to women in their forties. It happens to people who have used tampons for decades. It happens when you're exhausted, when your period tapers off and you forget you put one in, when you insert a new tampon without realizing the old one is still there, or when you have sex and a tampon gets pushed up high. None of this makes someone dirty or negligent.

Shame doesn't prevent TSS. Information does. So let's talk about what TSS really is.

## What Is Toxic Shock Syndrome?

Toxic shock syndrome is a rare but serious condition caused by toxins produced by certain bacteria, most commonly *Staphylococcus aureus* and sometimes *Streptococcus pyogenes*. Many people carry these bacteria on their skin or in their bodies without any problem. The trouble starts when the bacteria multiply and release toxins that enter the bloodstream.

Those toxins can trigger an overwhelming immune response. That's what makes TSS dangerous: it can lead to a rapid drop in blood pressure, organ failure, and, in rare cases, death.

## How Does It Develop?

TSS isn't only linked to tampons. It can occur after surgery, with skin wounds, burns, or after childbirth, and it can affect men and children. But menstrual TSS is the form most people have heard of.

When a tampon stays in the vagina for a long time, particularly a high-absorbency tampon, it can create an environment where bacteria thrive. Menstrual blood, warmth, and trapped oxygen can all contribute. Higher-absorbency products may also cause tiny abrasions in the vaginal walls, which may make it easier for toxins to enter the bloodstream.

Importantly, most people who have *S. aureus* do not develop TSS. Many adults have antibodies that protect them against the toxin. That's one reason TSS is more common in younger people, who may not have developed that protection yet.

## Why Early Symptoms Are Easy to Miss

Here's one of the hardest things about TSS: its early symptoms can look a lot like a normal rough period or a bad flu. They may include:

- Sudden high fever (often 102°F / 38.9°C or higher)
- Nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea
- Muscle aches
- Headache
- Dizziness or fainting, especially when standing
- A sunburn-like rash, often on the palms or soles
- Confusion
- Redness of the eyes, mouth, or throat

Many people already feel crampy, achy, tired, and a little queasy during their periods, so it's easy to brush these signs off. The key difference is that TSS symptoms tend to come on suddenly and escalate quickly. If you're using a tampon or other internal menstrual product and feel suddenly and seriously unwell, especially with a high fever, remove the product and seek medical care right away. Tell the care team you're menstruating and what product you're using.

## Why It Seemed to Disappear

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, TSS made headlines after a spike in cases linked to super-absorbent tampons. Those products were pulled from the market, absorbency labeling was standardized, and public awareness grew. Cases dropped dramatically.

Over time, TSS faded from public conversation, and people started assuming it was a thing of the past. Part of that impression also came from gaps in tracking. Surveillance of TSS became less consistent over the years, and not every case is reported. When a condition isn't closely tracked, it can seem to vanish even though it's still happening. TSS is rare, but it never went away.

## The Myth of the "Safe" Organic Tampon

I often hear patients say they switched to organic or 100% cotton tampons so they don't have to worry about TSS. I understand the appeal, and there may be other reasons someone prefers those products. But organic or all-cotton tampons do not eliminate the risk of TSS.

TSS is driven by bacteria and their toxins, not by whether the cotton was grown organically. Research has shown that the bacteria linked to TSS can grow on cotton as well as on synthetic materials. Absorbency and how long a product is left in place matter far more than the material.

The same principles apply to any internal product: use the lowest absorbency that meets your needs, change or empty it as directed, and follow the manufacturer's time limits.

## The Bottom Line

- TSS is rare, but it's real and can be serious.
- It's caused by bacterial toxins, and forgotten or long-worn internal products can raise the risk.
- Early symptoms can mimic a normal period or flu, so watch for sudden fever and feeling seriously unwell.
- Organic and all-cotton tampons are not risk-free.
- Forgetting a tampon is a common mistake, not a moral failure.

If you've ever forgotten a tampon, you're in very good company. What matters is knowing the signs and acting quickly if something feels wrong. So let's stop mocking people for being human and start sharing information that actually keeps each other safe.
