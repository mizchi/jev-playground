# Your Birth Control Deserves More Than a Quiz

If you've ever spent months, or years, cycling through birth control pills, patches, rings, and IUDs trying to find one that doesn't leave you with headaches, mood swings, weight changes, or breakthrough bleeding, you already know the truth: the way we choose contraception is broken.

For millions of people, finding the right birth control is a process of trial and error. You try a method. You wait three months to see if side effects settle. If they don't, you go back to your provider, switch to something else, and start the clock again. Some people spend years in this loop. Others give up on contraception altogether.

In response to that frustration, a cottage industry of online birth control quizzes has emerged. They promise to match you with the right method in a few clicks. It's an appealing idea. But after reviewing some of the most popular quizzes available, we at adyn found that they fall far short of what people actually need, and they reveal something bigger about how contraceptive care is delivered.

## Quizzes Are a Symptom, Not a Solution

Birth control quizzes didn't appear out of nowhere. They exist because the standard of care leaves people searching for answers on their own.

Today, contraceptive prescribing is driven largely by provider habit and convenience. A clinician might default to the pill they've prescribed most often, the one with samples in the cabinet, or the one they're most familiar with. Appointments are short, sometimes just a few minutes of counseling, and there's rarely time to explore a patient's full history, preferences, and biology in depth.

That's not a knock on individual providers, who are often working within tight constraints. It's a systemic problem. When the system doesn't offer personalization, people go looking for it elsewhere. Online quizzes fill that gap with the appearance of personalization, but without the substance.

## What We Found When We Reviewed Popular Birth Control Quizzes

We took a close look at widely used birth control quizzes from health websites, brands, and advocacy organizations. We answered them as different hypothetical users and compared what they asked and what they recommended. Five shortcomings stood out.

### 1. The Questions Are Inconsistent

There's no agreed-upon standard for what a birth control quiz should ask. Some focused almost entirely on lifestyle preferences, like whether you'd remember a daily pill. Others emphasized future pregnancy plans. A few asked about side effects you want to avoid, while others ignored side effects entirely.

The result? The same person can take three different quizzes and walk away with three different recommendations. If a tool's output depends more on who built it than on who's using it, it's not really personalizing anything.

### 2. The Prompts Are Confusing

Many quizzes used vague or clinical language without explanation. Questions like "Do you want a method that affects your cycle?" can mean very different things to different people. Does that mean lighter periods? No periods? More predictable timing?

Other questions assumed a level of medical knowledge many users don't have, such as asking whether someone prefers estrogen-containing methods without explaining what that means or why it matters. Confusing prompts lead to unreliable answers, and unreliable answers lead to unreliable recommendations.

### 3. They Don't Actually Narrow the Options

Perhaps the most surprising finding: many quizzes ended by recommending nearly every method. After answering a dozen questions, users were often shown a long list of "good matches" that included pills, patches, rings, shots, implants, and IUDs.

That's not guidance. It's a brochure. People turn to quizzes because they want help making a decision, and a list of nearly every option on the market doesn't move them any closer to one.

### 4. They Omit Critical Clotting Risk

This is the shortcoming that concerns us most. Estrogen-containing contraceptives, including many combined pills, the patch, and the ring, are associated with an increased risk of venous thromboembolism, or blood clots. For most people, that risk is low. But for some, it's significantly elevated due to personal or family history, smoking, age, migraines with aura, or inherited clotting conditions like Factor V Leiden.

Many of the quizzes we reviewed didn't ask about clotting history at all. Some didn't ask about smoking or migraines. That means a quiz could steer someone at high risk toward a method that could be genuinely dangerous for them. Any tool that claims to recommend birth control should, at minimum, screen for the factors that determine whether estrogen is safe.

### 5. They Can't Account for Individual Biology

Even the best-designed quiz has a hard ceiling: it can only work with the information you're able to self-report. It can't see how your body metabolizes hormones. It can't detect genetic variants that influence how you might respond to a particular method or your risk of certain side effects.

Two people with identical quiz answers can have very different experiences on the same pill. That's biology, and no amount of multiple-choice questions can capture it.

## What You Can Do Instead

The good news is that you don't have to settle for guesswork. Here are practical steps you can take to get more out of the process.

### Prepare for Your Counseling Appointment

Contraceptive counseling appointments are often brief, so walk in ready. Before your visit:

- **Write down your history.** Note any methods you've tried, how long you used them, and what side effects you experienced.
- **Know your family medical history.** In particular, find out whether any close relatives have had blood clots, strokes, or clotting disorders.
- **Clarify your priorities.** Is avoiding weight gain most important to you? Mood stability? Lighter periods? Not having to think about it daily? Rank what matters.
- **Be upfront about your lifestyle.** Smoking, migraines, and other health conditions all affect which methods are safe.
- **Ask direct questions.** Why is this method being recommended for me specifically? What would we try next if it doesn't work? What side effects should I watch for?

Coming prepared helps your provider make a more informed recommendation and signals that you expect a thoughtful conversation, not a default prescription.

### Seek Out Precision-Medicine Tools

Precision medicine has transformed fields like oncology and cardiology by using a patient's biology to guide treatment. There's no reason contraception should be left behind.

Tools like adyn's Birth Control Test combine genetic and hormonal insights with your health history to provide personalized information about how you may respond to different methods, including risk factors like clotting that quizzes routinely miss. Instead of starting from a provider's habit or a generic list, you and your provider can start from data about your body.

That doesn't replace the conversation with your clinician. It makes that conversation far more productive.

## The Bigger Picture: Women's Health Deserves Investment

Birth control has been widely available for more than 60 years, and yet the way we choose it has barely evolved. Tens of millions of people rely on contraception, and a large share of them will stop or switch methods because of side effects. Every one of those switches represents time lost, discomfort endured, and in some cases, unintended pregnancies.

The prevalence of online quizzes tells us something important: people are hungry for better tools. They want personalization. They want their individual experience to matter. And they're willing to seek it out on their own when the system doesn't provide it.

Meeting that demand requires investment in research, in diagnostics, and in innovation that treats women's health with the rigor it deserves. Historically, women's health has been underfunded and understudied, with conditions that primarily affect women receiving a fraction of the research dollars devoted to other areas. Contraception is one of the clearest examples of what that neglect looks like in everyday life.

We believe choosing a birth control method should be as precise as any other medical decision. It shouldn't depend on which quiz you happened to find or which pill your provider prescribes most often. It should depend on you.

A quiz can be a starting point for curiosity. But when it comes to your health, you deserve more than a quiz. You deserve a standard of care built around your biology.
