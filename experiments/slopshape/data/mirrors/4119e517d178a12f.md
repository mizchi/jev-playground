# How to Get Your Doctor to Take Your Period Pain Seriously

If you've ever doubled over from cramps, canceled plans because you couldn't get off the couch, or sat in a doctor's office being told "that's just part of being a woman," you are not alone. And your pain is real.

At Flex, we believe that talking openly about periods is how we make them better. For too long, period pain has been brushed off, minimized, or treated as something to quietly push through. That silence has real consequences: many menstruators wait years for answers, cycling through appointments before someone finally listens.

So let's talk about it. We turned to two OB/GYNs, Dr. Charis Chambers and Dr. Laura Laursen, for their guidance on why period pain is so often underestimated and, most importantly, what you can do to make sure your doctor hears you.

## Why Period Pain Gets Underestimated

First, let's be clear: some discomfort during your period is common. But common doesn't mean normal, and it definitely doesn't mean you have to suffer.

Part of the problem is that pain is subjective. There's no blood test that measures how much your cramps hurt, so providers rely heavily on how patients describe their experience. And many of us have been taught from a young age that period pain is something to downplay. We say "it's not that bad" when it is. We keep going to work or school because we think we're supposed to.

Dr. Chambers, who is well known for her work educating people about periods, often emphasizes that pain that disrupts your life is not something you should have to accept. When menstruators have been conditioned to minimize their symptoms, providers may walk away with an incomplete picture.

There's also a broader history at play. Research has long shown that women's pain, and particularly the pain of women of color, is more likely to be dismissed or undertreated in medical settings. Menstrual health has historically received less research attention and less time in medical training than many other areas of health. All of this adds up to a system where period pain is too easily brushed aside.

## Why Birth Control Is Often the Default Answer

If you've brought up painful periods with a doctor, there's a good chance you walked out with a prescription for hormonal birth control. And to be fair, there's a reason for that.

Hormonal birth control can be very effective at reducing period pain and heavy bleeding for many people. It's widely available, well studied, and often a reasonable first step.

But as both Dr. Chambers and Dr. Laursen point out, birth control can sometimes act as a Band-Aid. It may manage symptoms without identifying what's causing them. If an underlying condition is driving your pain, birth control might mask it for a while without truly addressing it, and some people don't want to take hormones, can't tolerate them, or find they simply don't work.

You deserve to understand *why* you're in pain, not just how to quiet it. It's completely okay to ask your provider questions like, "What might be causing this?" or "Are there tests we should consider?" or "What are my other options?"

## How to Prepare for Your Appointment

Walking into an appointment prepared can make a huge difference. Here's what our experts recommend.

### Describe How Pain Affects Your Life, Not Just a Number

You've probably been asked to rate your pain on a scale from 1 to 10. The trouble is, that number means different things to different people. Your "6" might be someone else's "9," especially if you've been living with pain for years and have learned to function through it.

Instead, both doctors encourage patients to focus on how pain affects their daily life. That's information a provider can really work with. Try saying things like:

- "I miss one to two days of work every month because of my period."
- "I can't exercise or leave the house on the first day of my cycle."
- "The pain wakes me up at night."
- "I've gone to urgent care because of how much it hurt."
- "I have to plan my social life around my period."

These concrete examples paint a clear, powerful picture of what you're experiencing.

### Log Exact Medication Doses and Timing

"I take ibuprofen" doesn't tell your doctor nearly as much as "I take 400 mg of ibuprofen every four hours starting the day before my period, and it barely helps."

Keep a record of:

- Which medications you take (over-the-counter or prescription).
- The exact dose.
- How often you take them.
- When you start taking them in relation to your period.
- How much relief they provide, if any.

This helps your provider understand whether you've been using medications effectively and whether your pain is responding to standard treatment. Pain that doesn't improve with appropriate doses of over-the-counter pain relievers is an important signal worth investigating further.

### Note Duration and Changes Over Time

How long does your pain last? Does it start before your period? Does it continue after bleeding stops? Has it gotten worse over the years?

Changes are especially important to mention. Pain that has gotten progressively worse, pain that's new, or pain that shows up at other times in your cycle, such as during sex, bowel movements, or urination, can point to specific conditions. Be as specific as you can.

### Track Your Cycles

A period tracker, whether it's an app, a calendar, or a simple notebook, is one of your most valuable tools. Over a few months, record:

- When your period starts and ends.
- How heavy your flow is (and how often you need to change your pad, tampon, cup, or disc).
- When pain begins and how severe it is.
- Any other symptoms, like nausea, fatigue, bloating, headaches, or mood changes.

Bringing this data to your appointment shows patterns that are hard to describe from memory, and it helps your provider take your concerns seriously.

## Conditions to Be Aware Of

Severe or worsening period pain can sometimes be a sign of an underlying condition. A few worth knowing about include:

**Endometriosis:** A condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus grows outside the uterus. It can cause intense period pain, pain during sex, pain with bowel movements or urination, and fertility challenges. Endometriosis is notoriously underdiagnosed, and many people wait years for a diagnosis.

**Fibroids:** Noncancerous growths in or on the uterus. Fibroids can cause heavy or prolonged bleeding, pelvic pressure, and pain. They're very common, and they're diagnosed at higher rates among Black women.

**Adenomyosis:** A condition in which the tissue lining the uterus grows into the muscular wall of the uterus. It can cause heavy, painful periods and an enlarged, tender uterus.

If any of these sound familiar, mention them to your provider and ask whether further evaluation, such as a pelvic exam, ultrasound, or referral to a specialist, might be helpful.

## Advocating for Yourself

Even with the best preparation, you might not feel heard. If that happens, remember: you're allowed to keep asking questions. You're allowed to bring a friend or partner for support. You're allowed to ask your doctor to document in your chart that you requested further testing. And you're allowed to seek a second opinion or find a provider who specializes in menstrual and pelvic health.

Advocating for yourself isn't being difficult. It's taking your health seriously, which is exactly what you deserve from your care team, too.

## You Deserve to Be Heard

Period pain is not something you have to quietly endure. Your experience matters, your symptoms are valid, and you deserve answers. By describing how pain affects your life, tracking your symptoms and medications, and asking thoughtful questions, you can walk into your next appointment feeling prepared and empowered.

At Flex, we'll keep talking about periods openly and honestly, because every menstruator deserves care that takes them seriously.
