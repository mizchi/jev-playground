# A Beginner's Guide to Common Gynecological Surgeries and Procedures

If you've ever sat in a doctor's office and heard words like "oophorectomy" or "colposcopy" tossed around, you're not alone in feeling a little lost. Gynecological medicine comes with a vocabulary all its own, and it can be intimidating, especially when the words are attached to your own body and health decisions.

The good news is that many gynecological procedures are routine, quick, and low-risk. Some are done in a clinic in under half an hour. Others are major surgeries that deserve careful thought and a real conversation with your provider. This guide walks through the most common ones in plain language so you can walk into your next appointment feeling more informed and less overwhelmed.

## What Is Gynecological Surgery?

Gynecological surgery refers to any surgical procedure involving the female reproductive system: the uterus, cervix, ovaries, fallopian tubes, vagina, and vulva. These procedures can be done to diagnose a problem, treat a condition, relieve symptoms, prevent disease, or provide permanent contraception.

Surgical approaches generally fall into a few categories:

- **Open (abdominal) surgery**, which uses a larger incision in the lower abdomen.
- **Laparoscopic surgery**, which uses a few small incisions and a camera. Recovery is usually faster.
- **Robotic-assisted surgery**, a form of laparoscopy where the surgeon controls precise robotic instruments.
- **Vaginal or hysteroscopic procedures**, which reach the reproductive organs through the vagina and cervix, often with no external incisions at all.

Which approach is used depends on the condition, your anatomy, your medical history, and your surgeon's expertise.

## Oophorectomy

**What it is:** An oophorectomy is the removal of one ovary (unilateral) or both ovaries (bilateral). It's often performed alongside removal of the fallopian tubes, called a salpingo-oophorectomy.

**Why it's done:** Common reasons include ovarian cysts or tumors, ovarian torsion (when an ovary twists and cuts off its blood supply), severe endometriosis, abscesses, and cancer. People with a high inherited risk of ovarian or breast cancer, such as those with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations, may choose a preventive oophorectomy.

**How it's performed:** Most oophorectomies are done laparoscopically, though open surgery may be needed for large masses or suspected cancer.

**Effects on fertility and hormones:** If one ovary is removed, the remaining ovary usually continues to release eggs and produce hormones, so fertility and hormonal function are often preserved. Removing both ovaries ends natural fertility and triggers immediate surgical menopause if you haven't already gone through menopause. That can bring hot flashes, sleep changes, vaginal dryness, and longer-term concerns about bone and heart health. Hormone therapy may be an option for some people.

**Key risks:** Bleeding, infection, damage to nearby organs, and anesthesia complications. For bilateral removal before natural menopause, the long-term health effects of early estrogen loss are an important part of the conversation.

## Hysterectomy

**What it is:** A hysterectomy is the removal of the uterus. It's one of the most common major surgeries performed on women in the United States. There are several types:

- **Total hysterectomy:** removal of the uterus and cervix.
- **Partial (supracervical) hysterectomy:** removal of the uterus while leaving the cervix.
- **Radical hysterectomy:** removal of the uterus, cervix, part of the vagina, and surrounding tissue, typically for cancer.

A hysterectomy may or may not include removal of the ovaries and tubes.

**Why it's done:** Fibroids, heavy or abnormal bleeding, endometriosis, adenomyosis, uterine prolapse, chronic pelvic pain, and gynecologic cancers.

**How it's performed:** Vaginally, laparoscopically, robotically, or through an abdominal incision. Minimally invasive approaches typically mean shorter hospital stays and faster recovery, often a few weeks rather than six to eight.

**Effects on fertility and hormones:** After a hysterectomy, you will no longer have periods and cannot become pregnant. If your ovaries are left in place, they generally continue producing hormones, so you won't enter menopause right away, though some research suggests menopause may arrive somewhat earlier.

**Key risks:** Bleeding, infection, blood clots, injury to the bladder or bowel, and reactions to anesthesia. Because it's a permanent and major surgery, it's worth asking whether less invasive alternatives might work for your situation.

## Fibroid Removal (Myomectomy)

**What it is:** Uterine fibroids are noncancerous growths in or on the uterus. A myomectomy removes the fibroids while leaving the uterus in place.

**Why it's done:** Fibroids can cause heavy periods, pelvic pressure, pain, frequent urination, and fertility problems. Myomectomy is often chosen by people who want to keep their uterus or preserve the possibility of pregnancy.

**How it's performed:** Depending on the size, number, and location of fibroids, a myomectomy can be done hysteroscopically (through the cervix, for fibroids inside the uterine cavity), laparoscopically, robotically, or through an abdominal incision.

**Effects on fertility and hormones:** Myomectomy is designed to preserve fertility and doesn't affect hormone production. Depending on how deeply the uterine wall was involved, your provider may recommend waiting a few months before trying to conceive and may advise a cesarean delivery in future pregnancies.

**Key risks:** Bleeding (sometimes significant), infection, scar tissue formation, and fibroid recurrence. New fibroids can grow over time, so some people need further treatment later.

## Endometrial Ablation

**What it is:** Endometrial ablation destroys the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) to reduce or stop menstrual bleeding.

**Why it's done:** It's primarily a treatment for heavy menstrual bleeding in people who are finished having children and haven't found relief with medication.

**How it's performed:** Ablation is typically done through the vagina and cervix with no incisions. Methods include heat, cold, radiofrequency energy, heated fluid, or microwave energy. Many ablations are outpatient procedures that take less than an hour, and most people return to normal activities within a few days.

**Effects on fertility and hormones:** Ablation does not affect your ovaries or hormones, but it is not meant for anyone who wants to become pregnant. Pregnancy after ablation is uncommon but possible, and it carries serious risks, so reliable contraception is still necessary afterward.

**Key risks:** Cramping, discharge, infection, and rarely, perforation of the uterus or burns to nearby organs. Some people find their bleeding returns over time.

## Tubal Ligation

**What it is:** Tubal ligation, often called "getting your tubes tied," is a permanent form of birth control that blocks, cuts, or removes the fallopian tubes so eggs can't meet sperm. Increasingly, surgeons perform a **bilateral salpingectomy**, removing the tubes entirely, which may also lower the risk of certain ovarian cancers.

**Why it's done:** For people who are certain they don't want future pregnancies.

**How it's performed:** Usually laparoscopically through one or two small incisions, often as an outpatient procedure. It can also be done shortly after childbirth or during a cesarean section.

**Effects on fertility and hormones:** Tubal ligation is intended to be permanent. Reversal is sometimes possible but is expensive, not always successful, and not an option after salpingectomy. The procedure doesn't affect your ovaries, so your hormones and periods continue as before. Any changes in your cycle may simply reflect stopping hormonal birth control.

**Key risks:** Anesthesia complications, infection, bleeding, and injury to nearby organs. If pregnancy does occur after the procedure, there's a higher chance it will be ectopic, which is a medical emergency.

## Diagnostic and Minor Procedures

Not every gynecological procedure involves an operating room. Many are quick, in-office tests that help your provider understand what's going on.

### Colposcopy

A colposcopy is a closer look at the cervix, vagina, and vulva using a magnifying instrument called a colposcope. It's usually recommended after an abnormal Pap smear or a positive HPV test. It feels similar to a Pap test, takes about 10 to 20 minutes, and doesn't require anesthesia. A vinegar-like solution may be applied to highlight abnormal cells.

### Cervical Biopsy

If something looks unusual during a colposcopy, your provider may take a small tissue sample from the cervix. This can cause brief cramping or a pinching sensation, and light spotting afterward is common. Some procedures, like a LEEP or cone biopsy, remove larger areas of abnormal tissue and can also serve as treatment. These may slightly increase the risk of preterm birth in future pregnancies, which is worth discussing if you plan to conceive.

### Trachelectomy

A trachelectomy is the removal of the cervix, usually as treatment for early-stage cervical cancer. A **radical trachelectomy** removes the cervix, surrounding tissue, and the upper part of the vagina while leaving the uterus in place. This makes it a fertility-sparing option for some patients. Pregnancy is possible afterward, but it's considered high-risk and typically requires a cesarean delivery. Unlike the other procedures in this section, a trachelectomy is major surgery.

### Cyst Removal (Ovarian Cystectomy)

Most ovarian cysts are harmless and go away on their own. When a cyst is large, painful, persistent, or looks suspicious on imaging, a surgeon may remove it through a cystectomy, usually laparoscopically. The goal is to take out the cyst while preserving the healthy ovary, which protects hormone production and fertility. Recovery typically takes one to two weeks.

## Talk It Through With Your Provider

Understanding the vocabulary is a great first step, but every body and every situation is different. The right choice depends on your symptoms, your health history, your plans for pregnancy, and what matters most to you.

Before any procedure, it's worth asking:

- Why is this procedure recommended for me?
- Are there less invasive alternatives?
- How will this affect my fertility and hormones?
- What does recovery look like?
- What are the risks in my specific case?

You deserve clear answers and a care plan that fits your life. Don't hesitate to ask questions, request a second opinion, or take time to decide. Your reproductive health is personal, and informed decisions are the best kind.
