# What the SEC's Expanded Accredited Investor Definition Means for CRE Fundraising

For decades, the definition of an "accredited investor" has shaped who can participate in private real estate deals. It determines which investors commercial real estate (CRE) sponsors can accept into their offerings and how they go about raising capital. So when the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) expanded that definition, it opened the door to a larger pool of potential investors and created new opportunities for sponsors.

To understand what the changes mean in practice, we sat down with Juniper Square's senior regulatory compliance counsel. She walked us through the regulatory history, the rules that govern private offerings, and why sponsors have reason to be optimistic.

## Starting at the Beginning: The Securities Act of 1933

**Why does the accredited investor definition matter in the first place?**

"It all goes back to the Securities Act of 1933," she explained. "After the stock market crash of 1929, Congress wanted to protect investors from fraud and make sure they had enough information to make informed decisions. The Securities Act requires that any offer or sale of securities be registered with the SEC unless an exemption applies."

Registering an offering is a lengthy and expensive process, involving extensive disclosures and ongoing reporting obligations. It's the path companies take when they go public, and it's generally impractical for a real estate sponsor raising capital for a single property or fund.

"That's why private offerings rely on exemptions," she said. "And the most widely used exemptions are found in Regulation D."

## Regulation D and the Safe Harbors

**How does Regulation D work for real estate sponsors?**

Regulation D provides a set of "safe harbors," rules that, if followed, give issuers confidence that their offering qualifies for an exemption from registration. For CRE sponsors, the two most important are Rule 506(b) and Rule 506(c).

"Rule 506(b) is the traditional approach," she said. "It allows a sponsor to raise an unlimited amount of money from an unlimited number of accredited investors, plus up to 35 non-accredited investors who are financially sophisticated. The tradeoff is that you can't engage in general solicitation. You can't advertise the offering publicly. You generally need a preexisting relationship with the investors you approach."

Rule 506(c), introduced after the JOBS Act of 2012, takes a different approach.

"Under 506(c), sponsors can advertise and publicly market their offerings," she explained. "But every investor must be accredited, and the sponsor has to take reasonable steps to verify that status. That might mean reviewing tax returns, bank statements, or getting a letter from an attorney or CPA."

In both cases, the accredited investor definition plays a central role. It determines who a sponsor can bring into a deal and, under 506(c), who they need to verify.

## The Existing Thresholds

**Before the changes, who qualified as an accredited investor?**

"For individuals, there were essentially two tests," she said. "The income test and the net worth test."

- **Income test:** An individual with income over $200,000 in each of the past two years, or joint income with a spouse over $300,000, with a reasonable expectation of reaching the same level in the current year.
- **Net worth test:** An individual with a net worth over $1 million, alone or together with a spouse, excluding the value of their primary residence.

Certain entities also qualified, including banks, insurance companies, registered investment companies, and trusts, corporations, and partnerships with more than $5 million in assets.

"The problem was that these thresholds were purely financial," she noted. "Wealth was treated as a proxy for sophistication. That left out a lot of people who clearly have the knowledge to evaluate a private investment, but might not meet the income or net worth tests."

## The New Categories

**What did the SEC change?**

The SEC's amendments added new ways for both individuals and entities to qualify, with a focus on financial knowledge and sophistication rather than wealth alone.

### Licensed Industry Professionals

"This is one of the most significant changes," she said. "Individuals who hold certain professional certifications or licenses, specifically the Series 7, Series 65, and Series 82 licenses, now qualify as accredited investors regardless of their income or net worth."

The SEC also left the door open to designating additional certifications in the future.

"The idea is simple: if you've demonstrated the expertise to advise others on investments, you should be able to invest yourself," she said.

### Spousal Equivalents

The amendments also updated how joint income and net worth are calculated.

"Previously, only married couples could combine their finances to meet the thresholds," she explained. "Now, spousal equivalents, meaning cohabitants in a relationship generally equivalent to a spouse, can pool their income and assets too. It's a modernization that reflects how many people actually live today."

### Family Offices

"Family offices with at least $5 million in assets under management now qualify, as long as they weren't formed specifically to invest in the offering and their investments are directed by someone with the knowledge and experience to evaluate the risks," she said. "Their family clients qualify as well."

For CRE sponsors, family offices are often an important source of capital, and this change removes uncertainty about their status.

### LLCs and Other Entities

"Limited liability companies were widely treated as accredited if they had more than $5 million in assets, but they weren't explicitly listed in the rule," she noted. "The amendments made that official."

The SEC also added a catch-all category for entities that own more than $5 million in investments and weren't formed to invest in the specific offering, including certain Native American tribes and government bodies.

In addition, "knowledgeable employees" of private funds now qualify to invest in their own firm's offerings.

## What It Means for CRE Sponsors

**Why should sponsors be excited about these changes?**

"The most obvious benefit is a larger investor pool," she said. "More people and entities qualify as accredited investors now. For sponsors, that means more potential investors to reach, particularly under 506(c), where you're marketing publicly."

She also pointed to the practical benefits for the subscription process.

"Clearer categories make it easier to understand who qualifies. A license can be verified quickly through FINRA's BrokerCheck, for example, which may be simpler than reviewing financial documents. Explicitly including LLCs and spousal equivalents reduces ambiguity. All of that can lead to smoother, simpler subscriptions for both sponsors and investors."

For sponsors using technology to manage their investor relationships and subscription workflows, these changes fit naturally into a digital process. Updated subscription documents and questionnaires can incorporate the new categories, helping investors self-identify accurately and helping sponsors keep clean records.

**Is there anything sponsors should watch out for?**

"Sponsors should make sure their subscription documents reflect the updated definition," she advised. "And under 506(c), the verification requirement still applies. The new categories make it easier to qualify, but sponsors still need to do their diligence. As always, work with your legal counsel to make sure you're compliant."

## Broadening Access to Real Estate Investing

The SEC's expanded definition of accredited investor is a meaningful step toward a more inclusive private investment market, one that recognizes knowledge and experience alongside wealth. For CRE sponsors, it means new opportunities to connect with qualified investors and raise capital more efficiently.
