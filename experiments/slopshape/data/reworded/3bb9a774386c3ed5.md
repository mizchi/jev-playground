# The Cloud-First Challenge: 6 Problems Tech Companies Must Solve in 2023

The cloud market is growing fast. Businesses of all sizes are moving workloads, applications, and whole tech stacks to the cloud, and spending on cloud services keeps rising every year. For engineering and business leaders, that means a large opportunity: faster innovation, more scalability, and the flexibility to respond as markets change.

Fast growth also creates real problems. A cloud-first tech stack takes more than a switch-over. It needs the right people, careful planning, and a clear grasp of the risks.

This article covers six of the biggest challenges companies face in building cloud-first tech stacks in 2023, and what leaders can do about each.

## What Is Cloud Computing?

Cloud computing is the delivery of computing services, including servers, storage, databases, networking, software, and analytics, over the internet. Rather than buying and maintaining physical hardware in their own data centers, companies rent these resources from cloud providers such as Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud, and they usually pay only for what they use.

The benefits are clear. Companies can add or remove resources as demand changes, bring new products to market faster, and spend less effort managing infrastructure.

## How the Pandemic Accelerated Cloud Adoption

Cloud adoption was already growing steadily before 2020, and the COVID-19 pandemic sped it up sharply. Within a very short time, businesses had to support remote workforces, move customer experiences online, and keep operating without access to physical offices.

The cloud made this possible. Collaboration tools, virtual desktops, ecommerce platforms, and remote access solutions all ran on cloud infrastructure. Companies that had put off migration found themselves speeding up plans once scheduled years out.

That momentum has continued. As cloud adoption matures, though, the difficulties of doing it well have become easier to see.

## Challenge 1: The Shortage of Cloud Engineering Talent

For many companies, the largest obstacle is finding people who can build and manage cloud infrastructure. Demand for cloud engineers, architects, DevOps specialists, and cloud security professionals is much greater than supply, and companies compete hard for experienced candidates.

There are three main ways to address the shortage:

**Hiring.** Looking beyond traditional candidate profiles can reveal new talent pools. Skills-based hiring judges candidates on what they can actually do instead of where they studied or which companies they've worked for, which helps teams find capable engineers they might otherwise miss. Technical assessments built around real-world cloud tasks make it easier to evaluate skills objectively.

**Retention.** Keeping the cloud talent you already employ matters as much as hiring more. Competitive pay, meaningful projects, flexible work arrangements, and clear paths for career growth all help keep engineers engaged.

**Upskilling.** Many organizations already employ talented developers and IT professionals who could move into cloud roles. Funding training, certifications, and hands-on learning builds cloud expertise in-house and shows employees that the company is investing in their future.

The most resilient organizations use all three.

## Challenge 2: Migration Is More Than Moving Data

Cloud migration can look like a simple transfer of data and applications from on-premises servers to the cloud. It is actually much more complicated.

A successful migration often means redesigning applications. Legacy systems may have to be refactored or rebuilt to use cloud-native features such as autoscaling, microservices, and managed services. Moving old applications over unchanged ("lifting and shifting") can result in poor performance and higher costs.

Migration also changes people and processes. Teams have to learn new workflows, tools, and ways of working together. Governance policies, security controls, and monitoring practices need updating. Meanwhile, the business has to keep operating with as little disruption as possible.

Leaders should approach migration as a strategic transformation rather than a purely technical project, with clear goals, phased timelines, and good communication between teams.

## Challenge 3: Vetting Vendors for Security and Compliance

Moving to the cloud means handing sensitive data and critical systems to third-party providers, so vetting vendors for security and compliance is essential.

Cloud security usually follows a shared responsibility model. The provider secures the underlying infrastructure, and the customer secures its own data, applications, access controls, and configurations. Confusion about where that boundary lies often leads to security gaps.

Before selecting a vendor, companies should assess:

- Security certifications and third-party audits.
- Compliance with relevant regulations and industry standards, such as GDPR, HIPAA, or SOC 2.
- Data encryption practices, both in transit and at rest.
- Incident response procedures and breach notification policies.
- Data residency options for organizations with geographic requirements.

The expanding set of SaaS tools and third-party services that connect to your cloud environment deserves the same scrutiny.

## Challenge 4: Planning for Backup and Redundancy

Cloud providers are very reliable, but outages still happen. Service disruptions, configuration errors, cyberattacks, and accidental deletions can all threaten data and operations.

For that reason, backup and redundancy planning is still critical. Companies should consider:

- **Regular, automated backups** stored in separate locations or regions.
- **Multi-region or multi-zone deployments** that keep services available if one area fails.
- **Disaster recovery plans** with clear recovery time and recovery point objectives.
- **Regular testing** to confirm that backups and failover processes work when needed.

Some organizations also adopt multi-cloud or hybrid strategies to depend less on a single provider, although these approaches bring their own complexity.

## Challenge 5: Rising Costs

Cost efficiency is one of the cloud's main attractions. Without careful management, however, cloud spending can grow out of control quickly.

Frequent causes include over-provisioned resources, idle instances, unused storage, data transfer fees, and poor visibility into who is spending what. As organizations grow, these costs accumulate quickly.

Many companies now use FinOps, a practice in which engineering, finance, and business teams manage cloud spending together. Its main strategies include:

- Monitoring usage and spending in real time.
- Right-sizing resources to match actual demand.
- Using reserved instances or savings plans for predictable workloads.
- Setting budgets and alerts to catch unexpected spikes.
- Making cost awareness part of engineering culture.

## Challenge 6: The Uncertain Impact of a Possible Recession

Many businesses are entering 2023 under economic uncertainty. Talk of a possible recession has leaders revisiting budgets, including technology budgets.

How this will affect cloud adoption is hard to say. Tighter budgets could slow or postpone some migration projects, and companies may examine cloud spending more closely. At the same time, the cloud's flexibility and pay-as-you-go model can help businesses reduce infrastructure costs and adjust quickly to new conditions, which could make it even more appealing in a downturn.

For many organizations, the probable outcome is a change in priorities: less focus on rapid expansion and more on optimization, efficiency, and getting full value from existing cloud investments.

## The Long-Term View: Cloud Adoption Is Inevitable

Cloud-first companies face real challenges in 2023: talent shortages, complex migrations, security, resilience, costs, and economic uncertainty. Each one is significant and calls for careful planning and strong leadership.

The long-term direction, however, is clear. The cloud now underpins modern technology, supporting everything from everyday business applications to advanced work in artificial intelligence and data analytics. Short-term obstacles may affect how fast adoption happens, but not where it is heading.

Companies that invest now in the right people, processes, and strategies will be in the strongest position in a cloud-first future.
