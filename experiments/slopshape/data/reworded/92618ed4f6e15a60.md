# How to Choose the Right Size Urn for Your Loved One's Ashes

Picking an urn is often among the first decisions a family faces after a death, and it can be a lot to take in. While grieving and handling arrangements, you may find yourself asking questions you've never considered: How much space do ashes take up? Should we keep them all together or divide them? What if we'd like to scatter some and keep some close?

There's no wrong way to honor someone you love. This guide covers the options step by step, from full-size urns down to small pieces of cremation jewelry, so your family can land on a choice that feels right.

## Why More Families Are Choosing Cremation

In the United States and many other countries, cremation is now the most common choice for families. People choose it for different, very personal reasons. Cost is one, since cremation usually costs less than a traditional burial. Flexibility is another: families can hold a memorial service at a time when everyone is able to gather, scatter ashes somewhere meaningful, or keep a loved one close at home.

Attitudes have shifted as well. Many people now feel less bound to traditional burial customs and prefer a memorial that reflects the particular life of the person they've lost. Cremation makes that kind of personalization possible, and the urn is a central part of it.

## Understanding the Basics: How Much Space Do Ashes Need?

The guideline most often used in the memorial industry is simple: **plan for about one cubic inch of urn capacity for every pound of healthy body weight** before passing. Someone who weighed 180 pounds, for example, would usually need an urn that holds around 180 cubic inches.

Round up when in doubt. A bit of extra room makes transferring the ashes easier and removes the worry that they won't fit. If you aren't sure of the volume, your funeral home or crematory can usually tell you roughly how much they returned to you.

Below, urn sizes are listed from largest to smallest.

## Standard Adult and Extra-Large Urns

**Typical capacity:** 180 to 250 cubic inches for standard adult urns; 250 cubic inches and up for extra-large urns

**Who they suit:** Families who want to keep all of their loved one's ashes together in one place

Standard adult urns are made to hold the complete cremated remains of most adults. Most are about 9 to 12 inches tall, but shapes vary a great deal: classic vases, boxes, sculptural pieces, and biodegradable urns meant for burial or water ceremonies.

Extra-large urns are meant for people who were taller or heavier, or for families who just want more room. They also serve as **companion urns**, which hold the ashes of two people, often spouses or partners who wanted to rest together. A companion urn usually holds 400 cubic inches or more and may have a single shared chamber or two separate compartments.

If the urn will go into a columbarium niche or be buried in a cemetery, confirm the niche dimensions or the cemetery's requirements before buying. Some cemeteries also require an urn vault for burial.

## Medium Urns

**Typical capacity:** Roughly 40 to 150 cubic inches

**Who they suit:** Small children, petite adults, beloved pets, or family members sharing a portion of ashes

Medium urns sit between full-size and keepsake urns. For a family who has lost an infant or young child, a medium urn often has the right capacity in a size that feels gentle and proportionate. Many designs in this range have a softer style, with delicate engravings or motifs suited to a little one.

Medium urns also work well when ashes are being shared. If a surviving spouse keeps most of the ashes, for example, an adult child might use a medium urn to keep a substantial portion at home. Many families also choose one when part of the ashes will be scattered and the rest kept.

## Tealight Urns

**Typical capacity:** Usually 3 to 15 cubic inches

**Who they suit:** Anyone who wants a small portion of ashes held in a comforting, everyday memorial

A tealight urn pairs a small sealed compartment for ashes with a holder for a tealight or small candle. It gives you a way to pause and remember: lighting the candle on a birthday, an anniversary, or at the end of a hard day can help you feel connected.

Since they hold only a little, tealight urns pair well with a full-size urn or can be part of a set shared among family members. They fit easily on a mantel, bookshelf, or bedside table.

## Keepsake Urns

**Typical capacity:** Generally 1 to 10 cubic inches

**Who they suit:** Families dividing ashes among several people, or anyone who wants a small, personal memorial

Keepsake urns are miniature versions of traditional urns and among the most popular ways to share ashes. When family members live in different cities, or several children and grandchildren each want something to hold on to, keepsakes let each of them keep part of your loved one nearby.

Many full-size urn designs are sold with **matching keepsake sets**. These suit families who want the pieces to belong together: one family member keeps the main urn, and the others receive matching keepsakes. Because every piece looks the same, the set reflects a shared love and a shared loss.

Keepsakes also make sense when most of the ashes will be scattered. Holding back a small portion in a keepsake gives you a place to return to, even if the rest lie in the ocean, a garden, or along a favorite mountain trail.

## Cremation Jewelry

**Typical capacity:** A tiny amount, often just a pinch of ashes

**Who they suit:** Those who want to carry their loved one with them every day

Cremation jewelry holds the smallest amount of ashes, and many people find it the most intimate option. Pendants, bracelets, rings, and charms are made with a small sealed chamber for a pinch of ashes. Some families instead choose pieces that hold a lock of hair, a little soil from a special place, or dried flowers from the service.

Designs run from simple hearts and teardrops to modern bars, cylinders, and custom-engraved pieces, commonly in stainless steel, sterling silver, or gold. Each piece holds so little that jewelry is easy to share, and many families buy several so everyone who wants one can have one.

A practical tip: most cremation jewelry includes a small funnel and a screw you can seal with a dab of adhesive so the ashes stay secure. Filling a piece can be emotional, so you might do it together or ask a funeral director to help.

## Combining Sizes to Meet Your Family's Needs

You aren't limited to a single type of urn. Many families find comfort in combining several, for example:

- A **full-size urn** for the primary caregiver or spouse
- **Keepsake urns** for each adult child
- **Cremation jewelry** for grandchildren or close friends
- A **tealight urn** for a shared family space

To plan, begin with the total volume of ashes and subtract what will go into keepsakes and jewelry. What's left determines the size of the main urn, or shows how much you'll have for scattering.

## A Gentle Next Step: Talking as a Family

Before you choose, it can help to sit down as a family and talk openly about what each person hopes for. Some questions to start with:

- Did your loved one express any wishes about their ashes?
- Will the ashes be kept together, shared among family members, or scattered?
- Where will the urn be kept, and who will care for it?
- Are there any cemetery, niche, or travel requirements to consider?

These talks can be emotional, so take the time you need. You don't have to decide by any deadline, and many families keep the ashes safely in a temporary container until they're ready.
