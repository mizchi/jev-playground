# What Is NFC, and How Does It Relate to RFID?

Tapping your phone on a terminal to pay for coffee is Near Field Communication at work. NFC is now one of the wireless technologies people use most often, but it's frequently mixed up with a close relative, Radio Frequency Identification (RFID). The two are connected without being interchangeable, and if you're picking a technology for your business, you need to know how they differ.

This post covers what NFC is, how it works, where it sits in the larger RFID family, and which enterprise uses suit each technology best.

## What Is Near Field Communication?

Near Field Communication is a group of short-range wireless standards that let two devices trade data once they're within a few centimeters of each other. Most modern smartphones now have NFC chips built in, and so do many tablets, wearables, and payment terminals.

The very short read range is by design. Since the devices have to be nearly touching, NFC comes with built-in security and a clear signal of intent: nothing happens unless someone chooses to tap.

## The Three NFC Transaction Modes

An NFC device can work in any of three modes:

**1. Peer to peer.** Two NFC-enabled devices trade information with each other directly. In early uses, people tapped phones together to share contacts, photos, or links. Both devices are active, and each can send and receive data.

**2. Read/write.** A device such as a smartphone reads data from, or writes data to, a passive NFC tag. Smart posters, product labels that open a web page, and tags that change phone settings or launch app actions all rely on this mode.

**3. Card emulation.** Here the NFC device acts like a contactless smart card. Mobile wallets such as Apple Pay and Google Pay run on this mode, as do phone-based transit passes and digital access badges.

## Clearing Up the Confusion: RFID Basics

The relationship between NFC and RFID makes more sense once you know how RFID works.

### The Core Components

An RFID system has three basic parts:

- **Chip:** A small integrated circuit that holds data, such as a unique identifier.
- **Antenna:** Connected to the chip, it sends and receives radio signals. The chip and antenna together make up an RFID tag or inlay.
- **Reader:** A device that emits radio waves to talk to tags and collect their data, which it often hands off to software for processing.

### Active vs. Passive Tags

There are two main kinds of RFID tags:

- **Active tags** carry their own battery and transmit signals either constantly or at set intervals. Their read range is long, sometimes hundreds of feet, but they're bigger and cost more. Companies typically use them to track high-value assets, vehicles, or shipping containers.
- **Passive tags** have no power source of their own. The reader's radio signal supplies enough energy to run the chip for a response. Because they're small, cheap, and durable, passive tags work well for high-volume uses like inventory and supply chain tracking.

### Frequency Bands

RFID systems use three main frequency ranges:

- **Low Frequency (LF), 125–134 kHz:** A short read range (up to about 10 cm) and good performance around metal and liquids. You'll find it in animal identification and older access control systems.
- **High Frequency (HF), 13.56 MHz:** A read range of about 10 cm to 1 m. It's used widely for library books, ticketing, payment cards, and access badges.
- **Ultra High Frequency (UHF), 860–960 MHz:** Read ranges of several meters or more, fast read rates, and the capacity to read hundreds of tags at the same time. It's the standard for retail inventory, logistics, and asset tracking.

### Where NFC Fits

Think of NFC as an extension of HF RFID. It runs at the same 13.56 MHz frequency and is built on HF RFID standards such as ISO/IEC 14443, with added protocols that let devices communicate in both directions. So every NFC system is a type of RFID, but RFID includes much more than NFC.

What sets NFC apart is its purpose: intentional, one-to-one exchanges at very close range. RFID as a category covers far more distances and use cases.

## Enterprise Takeaways: Choosing the Right Technology

Which technology a business should pick comes down to the job it needs done.

**NFC suits one-to-one interactions** in which a person deliberately taps a tag or device. In the enterprise, that often means:

- Employee access control and secure entry
- Event and transit ticketing
- Contactless payments
- Customer engagement, such as product authentication or tap-to-learn-more packaging
- Equipment check-in and check-out using staff smartphones

Because NFC-enabled phones are everywhere, many of these applications need no dedicated readers, which is a big advantage.

**Passive UHF RFID suits one-to-many, high-volume tracking**, where many items have to be identified quickly and at a distance. Common applications are:

- Retail and warehouse inventory management
- Supply chain and logistics visibility
- Asset tracking across large facilities
- Automated shipping and receiving

A single UHF RFID reader can pick up hundreds of tags in seconds without line of sight, giving you real-time visibility that NFC was never built to provide.
