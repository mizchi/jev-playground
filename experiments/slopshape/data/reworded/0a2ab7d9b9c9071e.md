# Forgot a Tampon? You're Not Careless. Let's Talk Honestly About TSS.

Toxic shock syndrome has been showing up everywhere in the news and on social media lately. Posts about people who forgot a tampon, in some cases for days, have gone viral, and the comments are harsh: "How do you forget something like that?" "That's just irresponsible." "Gross."

I'm an OB-GYN, and I want to be clear: forgetting a tampon is a normal human mistake, and I see it regularly in my practice. Teenagers do it, and so do women in their forties. People who have used tampons for decades do it. It happens when you're exhausted, when your period tapers off and you lose track of having put one in, when you insert a new tampon without realizing the old one is still in place, or when sex pushes a tampon up high. None of these situations mean someone is dirty or negligent.

Shaming people won't prevent TSS, but information will. So here is what TSS actually is.

## What Is Toxic Shock Syndrome?

Toxic shock syndrome is a rare but serious condition caused by toxins that certain bacteria produce, most often *Staphylococcus aureus* and occasionally *Streptococcus pyogenes*. Plenty of people carry these bacteria on their skin or in their bodies with no problems. The danger begins when the bacteria multiply and release toxins into the bloodstream.

Those toxins can set off an overwhelming immune response, which is why TSS is so dangerous: blood pressure can drop quickly, organs can fail, and in rare cases the person can die.

## How Does It Develop?

Tampons aren't the only link to TSS. It can also occur after surgery, childbirth, skin wounds, or burns, and men and children can get it. Menstrual TSS, however, is the form most people know about.

A tampon left in the vagina for a long time, especially a high-absorbency one, can create conditions where bacteria thrive. Menstrual blood, warmth, and trapped oxygen can all play a part. Higher-absorbency products may also cause tiny abrasions in the vaginal walls, possibly giving toxins an easier route into the bloodstream.

Keep in mind that most people who carry *S. aureus* never develop TSS. Many adults have antibodies that protect them from the toxin, which is one reason TSS shows up more often in younger people who may not have built that protection yet.

## Why Early Symptoms Are Easy to Miss

One of the hardest things about TSS is that its early symptoms can resemble an ordinary rough period or a bad flu. They may include:

- Sudden high fever (often 102°F / 38.9°C or higher)
- Nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea
- Muscle aches
- Headache
- Dizziness or fainting, especially when standing
- A sunburn-like rash, often on the palms or soles
- Confusion
- Redness of the eyes, mouth, or throat

Since many people already feel crampy, achy, tired, and slightly nauseated on their periods, these signs are easy to dismiss. What sets TSS apart is that the symptoms usually start suddenly and worsen fast. If you're using a tampon or another internal menstrual product and suddenly feel seriously ill, especially with a high fever, take the product out and get medical care immediately. Let the care team know you're menstruating and which product you're using.

## Why It Seemed to Disappear

TSS drew headlines in the late 1970s and early 1980s, when cases spiked in connection with super-absorbent tampons. Those products came off the market, absorbency labeling became standardized, and public awareness increased. Case numbers fell sharply.

As the years passed, people stopped talking about TSS and began treating it as a thing of the past. Gaps in tracking added to that impression: TSS surveillance grew less consistent over time, and not every case gets reported. A condition that isn't closely monitored can look like it has disappeared while it keeps occurring. TSS is rare, but it never went away.

## The Myth of the "Safe" Organic Tampon

Patients often tell me they switched to organic or 100% cotton tampons so they wouldn't need to worry about TSS. I understand why that's appealing, and people may have other good reasons for preferring those products. Organic or all-cotton tampons still do not eliminate TSS risk.

What drives TSS is bacteria and their toxins, not whether the cotton was grown organically. Research has found that TSS-linked bacteria grow on cotton as well as on synthetic materials. A product's absorbency and how long it stays in place matter much more than what it's made of.

The same rules hold for any internal product: choose the lowest absorbency that works for you, change or empty it as directed, and stay within the manufacturer's time limits.

## The Bottom Line

- TSS is rare, but it's real and can be serious.
- Bacterial toxins cause it, and internal products that are forgotten or worn for a long time can increase the risk.
- Early symptoms can look like a normal period or the flu, so watch for a sudden fever and feeling seriously unwell.
- Organic and all-cotton tampons still carry risk.
- Forgetting a tampon is a common mistake, not a moral failure.

If you've ever forgotten a tampon, plenty of people have done the same. The important part is recognizing the signs and acting fast if something feels wrong. Let's stop mocking people for being human and start sharing information that actually keeps us all safe.
