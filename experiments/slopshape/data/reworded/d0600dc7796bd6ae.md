# ABLE Accounts vs. Special Needs Trusts: A Comparison for Fiduciaries

The ABLE Act became law in 2014, and since then ABLE accounts have let individuals with disabilities save without endangering their eligibility for means-tested benefits such as Supplemental Security Income (SSI) and Medicaid. Few people use them, though. Only a small fraction of the millions of Americans estimated to be eligible have opened an account. For fiduciaries, including trustees, financial advisors, and attorneys, the gap is a missed opportunity and a reason to learn how ABLE accounts compare with the more familiar Special Needs Trust (SNT).

## Eligibility and Age of Disability Onset

**ABLE accounts** are open only to individuals whose disability began before a set age. At present, the disability must have started before age 26. The ABLE Age Adjustment Act raises that limit to age 46 beginning January 1, 2026, making millions more people eligible, among them many veterans and people with later-onset conditions. The beneficiary must also be entitled to SSI or SSDI on the basis of disability, or meet the disability criteria through a certification.

**Special Needs Trusts** carry no age-of-onset requirement. A first-party SNT generally must be set up for a beneficiary under age 65. Third-party SNTs, which are funded with assets that belong to someone other than the beneficiary, can be created at any age.

## Beneficiary Control and Independence

Control is one of the biggest differences between the two tools.

The beneficiary of an **ABLE account** can often run it personally: making contributions, choosing investments, and directing distributions. That arrangement can build independence, financial literacy, and dignity. If the beneficiary can't manage the account, an authorized legal representative may do it instead.

A trustee manages a **Special Needs Trust** and decides on distributions at their discretion, and the beneficiary cannot reach the trust funds directly. The structure protects beneficiaries who may be exploited or who can't manage money, at the cost of their autonomy.

## Contribution and Balance Limits

Annual contributions to an **ABLE account** are capped at an amount tied to the federal gift tax exclusion. Under the ABLE to Work provision, employed beneficiaries who don't participate in an employer retirement plan may contribute extra earnings. Total balances are capped at each state's 529 plan limit. SSI disregards only the first $100,000 in an ABLE account, and a balance above that amount may lead to suspension of SSI benefits.

**Special Needs Trusts** have neither annual contribution limits nor a maximum balance, which makes them a good fit for large sums such as inheritances, personal injury settlements, or substantial family gifts.

## Housing Costs and SSI

On housing, ABLE accounts have a clear edge.

**ABLE account** distributions for housing expenses, including rent, mortgage payments, property taxes, and utilities, count as qualified disability expenses and leave SSI benefits untouched.

When a **Special Needs Trust** pays shelter costs directly, the payment is generally treated as in-kind support and maintenance and can lower the beneficiary's monthly SSI benefit. Trustees have to decide whether the housing payment is worth the reduction in benefits.

## Tax Treatment and Funding Assets

Earnings in an **ABLE account** grow tax-free, and distributions spent on qualified disability expenses are exempt from federal income tax. Some states also let contributors deduct contributions from state income tax. ABLE accounts accept only cash contributions, however.

**Special Needs Trusts** can hold many kinds of assets, including real estate, securities, life insurance proceeds, and business interests. Their tax treatment depends on the trust's structure: trust income may be taxed at compressed trust tax rates unless the trust qualifies for grantor trust treatment or a qualified disability trust exemption.

When the beneficiary dies, both ABLE accounts and first-party SNTs are generally subject to Medicaid payback, although states differ in how they enforce ABLE payback. Third-party SNTs are exempt from Medicaid payback.

## Working Together

Fiduciaries don't have to choose between an ABLE account and a Special Needs Trust; the two often work best in combination. An SNT can hold large or non-cash assets and fund an ABLE account from time to time, so the beneficiary can handle everyday expenses, housing included, more independently and without losing SSI.

With the 2026 age expansion approaching, fiduciaries can do much to close the enrollment gap. The rules for these tools are complex and differ from state to state, so individuals and families should consult a qualified special needs planning attorney about the right strategy for their situation.
