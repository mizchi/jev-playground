# 5 Lessons Nonprofits Learned in a Year Like No Other

The pandemic overturned almost every plan nonprofits had made, and organizations had to rethink how they communicate, raise money, hold events, and support the people behind their missions. Galas were canceled. Volunteer shifts vanished overnight. Staff members did the work of three people.

Many nonprofits still did more than survive. They learned, adapted, and emerged with a clearer view of their priorities.

We asked Giveffect clients about the biggest lessons of this difficult year. They answered candidly and offered plenty of practical advice. Below are five lessons that stood out, with suggestions for applying each at your own organization.

## Lesson 1: Personalized Communication Makes All the Difference

> "We realized our supporters don't all want the same message. Segmenting our lists changed everything."

> "People responded when we spoke to them like individuals, not a mailing list."

With in-person contact gone, nonprofits relied mainly on email, phone calls, and social media to reach supporters. Many soon found that sending everyone the same message didn't work.

A first-time donor, a longtime major gift supporter, a volunteer, and an event attendee each relate to your organization differently. Their interests differ, and so do the kinds of outreach they respond to.

**How to put it into practice:**

- **Segment your constituents.** Group supporters by giving history, engagement level, interests, location, or how they first found you.
- **Tailor your messages.** A lapsed donor might need a reminder of the impact their past gifts made possible. A loyal monthly donor might enjoy a behind-the-scenes update.
- **Use personal details thoughtfully.** Using people's names and mentioning their past involvement shows that you know them.
- **Start small.** If a small team finds segmentation daunting, start with two or three groups and add more later.

## Lesson 2: Plan Campaigns Early, and Leave Room to Revise

> "Starting our year-end campaign in the summer gave us breathing room when things inevitably changed."

> "We built in time to brainstorm and rework our plans. That flexibility saved us."

Last-minute campaigns are stressful in any year. Amid this year's uncertainty, they became close to impossible. Clients who began planning months ahead had room to adjust when conditions changed.

Planning early doesn't mean fixing every detail. It means laying a solid foundation that still leaves space for new ideas and revisions.

**How to put it into practice:**

- **Start three to six months out.** For major campaigns such as year-end giving or annual appeals, begin planning well before launch.
- **Schedule brainstorming sessions.** Gather staff, board members, and even key volunteers to come up with ideas.
- **Build in checkpoints.** Set regular times to review the plan and decide what needs to change.
- **Prepare backup plans.** Decide in advance what you'll do if an event can't go ahead as planned or a key message falls flat.
- **Map out your communications calendar.** A stretched team scrambles less when it knows what goes out and when.

## Lesson 3: Stay Flexible With Event Formats, and Put Safety First

> "Our virtual gala reached people we never would have seen in a ballroom."

> "We learned to plan for in-person, virtual, and hybrid all at once. It was a lot, but it worked."

> "Keeping our community safe had to come before everything else."

For many nonprofits, events are central to fundraising and community building. Once gatherings became unsafe, organizations turned to virtual galas, online auctions, drive-through events, and hybrid formats.

Many clients were surprised that virtual events broadened their reach, drawing supporters from across the country who could never have come in person. Others found that small, safe in-person gatherings helped people stay connected.

**How to put it into practice:**

- **Consider every format.** Decide whether your event could work virtually, in person, or as a hybrid.
- **Prioritize safety.** Follow public health guidance and explain your safety measures clearly so attendees feel at ease.
- **Invest in the virtual experience.** Online, good audio, engaging hosts, interactive elements, and a clear run of show matter a great deal.
- **Keep what works.** Even as in-person events come back, virtual components can widen your reach and make it easier to participate.
- **Stay ready to pivot.** Have a plan for switching formats if conditions change shortly before the event.

## Lesson 4: Donor Retention Is the Heart of Fundraising

> "Our loyal donors carried us through. We should have been focusing on them all along."

> "It's so much easier to keep a donor than to find a new one."

With events canceled and new donors harder than ever to find, many nonprofits relied on their existing supporters. An overwhelming number of clients said donor retention became their top priority, and many wished they had focused on it earlier.

Keeping donors costs far less than acquiring new ones, and loyal donors often give more over time. They are also more likely to become volunteers, advocates, and even legacy givers.

**How to put it into practice:**

- **Thank donors quickly and sincerely.** A prompt, personal thank-you starts a lasting relationship on the right footing.
- **Show impact.** Share stories, photos, and updates that show what gifts are accomplishing.
- **Stay in touch between asks.** Some of your messages should ask for nothing.
- **Track retention rates.** Know how many donors give again from one year to the next, and set goals for improving that number.
- **Reach out to lapsed donors.** A thoughtful message can often revive a relationship.
- **Encourage recurring giving.** Monthly donors provide steady, predictable support.

## Lesson 5: Support Volunteers With Flexibility and Genuine Appreciation

> "Letting volunteers choose their own shifts made a huge difference in how many showed up."

> "A simple, heartfelt thank-you meant more to our volunteers than we realized."

During the pandemic, volunteers dealt with health concerns, caregiving responsibilities, and changing work schedules. Nonprofits that gave volunteers more say over their time and thanked them sincerely kept their volunteer communities strong.

**How to put it into practice:**

- **Give volunteers schedule control.** Let them sign up for shifts that suit their lives, and make changing plans easy.
- **Offer remote opportunities.** Virtual tasks such as making calls, writing notes, or handling administrative work let people stay involved from home.
- **Acknowledge contributions sincerely.** Personalized thank-you notes, social media shout-outs, and volunteer spotlights help people feel valued.
- **Check in regularly.** Ask volunteers how they're doing and what would improve their experience.
- **Celebrate milestones.** Recognize hours served, years of involvement, and special contributions.
