# People Before Metrics: Rethinking Disaster Preparedness in Outsourcing

Hurricane Otis hit Acapulco in October 2023 with almost no warning. Over roughly half a day, it grew from a tropical storm into a Category 5 hurricane, the strongest ever to make landfall on Mexico's Pacific coast. It destroyed homes, cut power and communications, and left families searching the wreckage for loved ones.

Companies with teams in the path of storms like Otis immediately face pressing questions. How fast can operations restart? How much will service be disrupted? What happens to service-level agreements?

Those questions are reasonable, but they shouldn't come first. The first question should always be: *Are our people safe?*

At Boldr, we believe disaster preparedness for outsourced operations must begin with people rather than continuity metrics. Look after the people doing the work, and continuity comes with it. Neglect them, and a plan on paper won't last long.

## The Reality of Disruption in a Global Workforce

Global outsourcing lets companies hire extraordinary talent in the Philippines, Mexico, Kenya, South Africa, and other countries. It also means working in regions that face many kinds of risk, and preparing responsibly starts with understanding those risks.

### Natural Disasters

Several of the world's busiest outsourcing hubs are also among the places most exposed to disaster. The WorldRiskIndex, which measures how exposed and vulnerable countries are to extreme natural events, has repeatedly placed the Philippines at the top as the world's highest-risk country, with Mexico close behind.

An average of around 20 typhoons hit the Philippines each year. Typhoon Haiyan (known locally as Yolanda) killed more than 6,000 people in 2013, and in December 2021 Typhoon Rai (Odette) devastated large parts of the Visayas and Mindanao, leaving some areas without power and connectivity for weeks. Because the country lies on the Pacific Ring of Fire, it also faces earthquakes and volcanic eruptions, such as Taal Volcano's 2020 eruption near Manila.

Mexico has its own combination of hazards: hurricanes on both coasts, flooding, and strong earthquakes, including the magnitude 7.1 quake that hit central Mexico in September 2017, killing hundreds and damaging buildings throughout Mexico City.

Climate change is making extreme weather more intense, so these risks are increasing.

### Political Unrest

Political instability can halt operations as thoroughly as a storm. Myanmar's 2021 military coup brought internet shutdowns, protests, and violence that disrupted daily life and business. Sri Lanka's economic crisis in 2022 led to mass protests, fuel shortages, and power cuts. Around the world, elections, civil demonstrations, and abrupt policy changes can bring curfews, transportation disruptions, or communications blackouts.

### Industrial Accidents

Other disruptions are man-made and arrive with no warning. The 2020 explosion at the port of Beirut killed more than 200 people, injured thousands, and damaged large parts of the city. In 2013, the Rana Plaza building collapse in Bangladesh killed more than 1,100 garment workers and showed how fragile global supply chains are, and how much they depend on people. Fires, chemical spills, and infrastructure failures can happen suddenly and affect entire communities.

## Why People-First Preparedness Matters

Conventional business continuity planning tends to concentrate on systems: backup servers, redundant internet connections, alternative sites, and recovery time objectives. We invest in these because they matter, but they leave out something essential.

Each outsourced operation consists of people with families, homes, and communities. In a disaster, they're living through the same crisis as their neighbors. Expecting them to log in before they know their family is safe, or while their home is underwater, is both unrealistic and wrong.

Putting people first means treating team members' well-being as the basis of any continuity plan that can last. Team members who feel supported in a crisis tend to return to work sooner, stay with the company longer, and do exceptional work for clients. Caring for people makes the operation more resilient.

Boldr is a certified B Corporation, committed to balancing purpose and profit and to weighing how our decisions affect workers, communities, and the environment. Disaster preparedness puts those values to one of their hardest tests.

## How Boldr Puts People First in Business Continuity

These are the practices that guide how we prepare for disruption and how we respond to it.

### Employee Check-Ins and Tracking

After a major storm, earthquake, or other emergency, our first job is to reach every team member. Structured check-ins confirm that each person is safe and tell us their circumstances: whether they have power, internet, shelter, and access to essentials.

We monitor responses carefully, follow up with anyone who hasn't replied, and escalate when necessary. Our leaders then have a clear, real-time view of who is safe, who needs help, and who can work. Operational planning comes only after that.

### Support for Affected Staff

Identifying who needs help is just the start. We give practical support to team members hit by disasters, which can include emergency financial assistance, relief goods, help finding temporary accommodation, and flexible time off.

A disaster's effects also last beyond the storm itself. Rebuilding a home, looking after family, or dealing with trauma can take weeks or months, so we aim to give people the time and support they need to recover, including mental health resources where we can.

### Training and Certifications

Preparation begins long before a disaster arrives. We fund training in first aid, emergency response, and evacuation procedures so team members can act safely and confidently in an emergency. Our leaders receive crisis management training, and we regularly check our business continuity practices against recognized standards.

People who know what to do are safer and calmer, and they're better able to help one another and their communities.

### Clear, Honest Client Communication

Clients are owed transparency, particularly in a crisis. From the moment we learn of a possible threat, we keep them updated on what we know, what we're doing to protect our teams, and how service is likely to be affected.

When disruptions are coming, we say so, and we build realistic plans with clients to handle them. We've found that clients who understand and share our values value a partner that puts people first, and many have gone out of their way to help affected teams themselves.

### Debriefs and Continuous Improvement

Following each significant event, we run debriefs on what worked and what could be better. We collect feedback from team members, leaders, and clients and apply the lessons to our plans.

Every disaster is different, and preparedness is never complete. Each event teaches us something and leaves us better prepared for the next.

### Cross-Country Talent Redundancy

Keeping a client's work out of a single location is one of the best ways to protect both people and operations. Because Boldr has teams in several countries, we can spread work so that when one site is affected, colleagues in other locations can take on part of the load.

With that redundancy, nobody has to pick between their family's safety and their job. Team members in the affected area can concentrate on recovery while colleagues in another country keep critical work going. Continuity rests on shared responsibility instead of pressure.

## The Wider Impact: Communities, Not Just Companies

Done well, outsourcing can do a great deal of good. It creates meaningful jobs, strengthens local economies, and helps families build more secure futures. That good only holds up if companies support their people when it counts most.

In a disaster, a business can treat the affected region as a risk to manage or as a community to support. Boldr chooses to support. Our teams have organized relief drives, volunteered in recovery work, and helped neighbors in need. When companies invest in their people, the benefits spread into the surrounding community.

We think the outsourcing industry as a whole has both the opportunity and the responsibility to lead on this. Continuity metrics will always matter, but they should result from a thoughtful, human-centered approach rather than replace one.
