# Leaked Credentials: Why They Matter and How Castle Is Helping You Protect Your Users

Hardly a week passes without news of another data breach at a retailer, a social network, a gaming platform, or a SaaS tool. Millions of email addresses and passwords leak out, and most people shrug it off. If the breach wasn't at their bank, why should they care?

The reason is a very common habit: password reuse. That habit is exactly what has made leaked credentials databases one of the most powerful tools attackers have.

## What Is a Leaked Credentials Database?

A leaked credentials database collects usernames, email addresses, and passwords exposed in data breaches. The passwords may be in plain text, or they may be hashed so weakly that attackers can crack them fast. In both cases, the data spreads quickly once it escapes.

The path usually runs like this:

1. **The breach.** An attacker gets into a company's systems and exfiltrates user data.
2. **The dark web.** The stolen data is often sold privately on underground marketplaces or shared in closed forums, where fresh data fetches a premium.
3. **The spread.** The data is resold and traded repeatedly, and it gets merged with other breaches into huge "combo lists" holding hundreds of millions, or even billions, of credential pairs.
4. **The public forums.** Much of it eventually becomes free to download on hacking forums, file-sharing sites, and messaging channels, open to anyone who goes looking.

By then, attackers have used, reused, and weaponized the credentials many times.

## Why Password Reuse Makes Leaks So Dangerous

If every person used a different password on every site, a breach would mostly harm only the breached company. People don't work that way. Studies consistently find that a large majority of users reuse passwords across multiple accounts, often changing them only slightly.

Reuse is why credential stuffing works so well. Attackers feed leaked email and password pairs into automated tools that try them on login pages across the internet. Only a small percentage of attempts need to succeed: with lists this large, a single campaign can compromise thousands of accounts.

The account that falls may not belong to the company that was breached. It could be on your platform. A user whose credentials leaked from a forum they joined a decade ago may have used the same password with you, and your login system can't tell the attacker from the real user.

## The White Hats Fighting Back

The security community has responded. Troy Hunt's **Have I Been Pwned** is now the standard resource for people who want to know whether their email address appears in a known breach. Its Pwned Passwords service lets developers check whether a password has been exposed without ever revealing the password.

Password managers such as **1Password** build breach monitoring into their products through features like Watchtower, which alerts users when their credentials appear in a known leak and prompts them to update weak or reused passwords.

These efforts have helped enormously, but they all rely on users to act. Most people never visit Have I Been Pwned, many don't use a password manager, and only a small fraction monitor their own exposure.

## Your Platform Is the Guardian

Customer-facing websites and applications can fill that gap. A user who opens an account on your platform trusts you to keep it safe. Fair or not, if an attacker gets in, the user will blame you, even when the password leaked from an entirely different site.

That makes you responsible for guarding your users' security. You can see risk that users can't or won't see themselves, and you can intervene before an account is compromised. To do that, you need to know which of your users have credentials circulating in leaked databases.

## Introducing Castle's Leaked Credentials Database

Castle now maintains its own aggregated database of leaked credentials, compiled from a wide range of known breaches and public dumps. That data is already working behind the scenes to protect your users.

### Smarter Risk Scoring

Castle's risk scoring now uses breach exposure as a signal. At login, Castle checks whether a user's credentials have appeared in known leaks and weighs that alongside the behavioral and device signals it already analyzes. A login from an unfamiliar device with credentials known to be exposed differs sharply from a routine login on a trusted laptop, and Castle's risk assessment accounts for the difference.

As a result, Castle decides more accurately when to challenge a user with step-up authentication, when to block a suspicious attempt, and when to let a legitimate user in without friction.

### New Dashboard Features

The Castle dashboard also has new features that show your team breach exposure across your user base:

- **Exposure flags.** User profiles clearly flag anyone whose credentials have appeared in leaked databases.
- **Filtering.** You can filter your user list to see exactly which users are exposed, so you can decide whom to contact or investigate first.
- **Context at a glance.** While reviewing suspicious activity, your team can see immediately whether breach exposure may play a role.

With that information, you can act ahead of an attack: prompt exposed users to reset their passwords, encourage them to turn on multi-factor authentication, or watch their accounts more closely.

### No Integration Changes Required

You don't have to change anything. If you already send login events to Castle, breach exposure data flows into risk scoring and appears in your dashboard automatically. There are no new SDKs, no new API calls, and no engineering sprint.
