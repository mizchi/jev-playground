# From Product-Centric to Customer-Centric: How Industrial Manufacturers Can Unlock Cross-Sell Growth With Better Data

Industrial machinery and equipment manufacturers have spent decades organizing around their products. Business units follow product lines, sales teams earn commission on specific portfolios, and customer relationships are frequently handled in silos. The model has worked well for the industry, but it misses a significant amount of revenue.

A customer that buys compressors from one division may also need pumps, service contracts, replacement parts, and digital monitoring solutions sold by three other divisions. In many organizations, nobody sees that customer as a whole, so the cross-sell opportunity is never spotted.

Manufacturing executives are no longer asking whether to become more customer-centric. They are asking how to do it without disrupting the business, and the first step is data.

## The Power of Combining Internal and External Customer Data

Most manufacturers already have a lot of customer information, spread across ERP systems, CRM platforms, service management tools, dealer portals, and warranty databases. Each system holds only a piece of the picture, though, and one customer can show up dozens of times with slightly different names, addresses, and account numbers.

Once that internal data is consolidated and enriched with external sources such as firmographic data, corporate hierarchies, industry classifications, and installed-base intelligence, manufacturers get a much clearer view. They can see:

- Which customers buy from several business units and which buy from just one
- How parent companies and subsidiaries connect across regions
- Where competitors' equipment sits at customer sites
- Which accounts have buying patterns suggesting they are ready for complementary products

With this unified view, cross-selling stops depending on individual relationships and becomes a repeatable strategy grounded in data.

## The Hurdles Executives Must Plan For

The shift is seldom straightforward. Across our work with industrial manufacturers, the same four challenges keep coming up.

**Building trustworthy golden records.** A customer-centric strategy needs one authoritative record per customer. Producing that golden record means matching, merging, and validating data from many sources. Sales teams will ignore data they don't trust.

**Overcoming cultural resistance to centralization.** Business units that have owned their customer relationships for a long time may see a centralized customer master as losing control. Executives need to sponsor the effort and explain plainly how everyone benefits.

**Crediting product business units fairly.** If one division finds a cross-sell opportunity for another, which one gets credit? Collaboration stalls when attribution and incentive models are unclear, so organizations have to decide how revenue and opportunities will be shared.

**Sustaining data stewardship.** Master data is an ongoing commitment, not a one-time project. Customers merge, move, and restructure. Without continuing governance, assigned ownership, and stewardship processes, data quality declines and the golden record becomes less useful.

## Client Engagement: A Large Equipment Manufacturer

A global heavy equipment manufacturer came to Infoverity with a common problem. Its product divisions ran independently, and each kept its own customer data. Leadership believed many customers overlapped across divisions but couldn't reliably verify it.

In a roadmapping engagement, Infoverity assessed data across multiple ERP and CRM systems. The team then designed a customer master data strategy with matching rules, external data enrichment, and a hierarchy model that tied subsidiaries to their parent accounts. The analysis showed that a substantial share of customers bought from only one division even though their needs matched what other divisions offered.

Once the roadmap and governance model were established, the manufacturer was ready to launch coordinated account strategies and show sales teams opportunities across divisions.

## Client Engagement: An Oilfield Services Firm

An oilfield services company had its own version of the problem. Its customer data was split across service lines and geographies, so it was hard to see the full extent of its relationships with major operators.

Infoverity worked with the firm to define a unified customer data model and bring in external data that clarified corporate hierarchies across operators, joint ventures, and regional entities. The engagement also set up stewardship processes and ownership so the data would stay accurate as customers changed.

The firm ended up with a consolidated view of its customer relationships, which let leadership find underserved accounts and pursue bundled service offerings with more confidence.

## Making the Shift

Shifting from product-centric to customer-centric selling changes the organization as much as it changes the technology. It depends on trusted data, aligned incentives, and ongoing governance. Manufacturers that build these foundations can find revenue they were missing, deepen customer relationships, and compete better in a market that keeps getting more demanding.
