# Closing the Gaps: How PBMs, Pharmacies, and Providers Can Tackle Medication Nonadherence

Medication nonadherence is still one of the most stubborn and expensive problems in American healthcare. Decades of research, new therapies, and wider awareness have not changed the basic figure: roughly half of patients with chronic conditions do not take their medications as prescribed. The effects reach every part of the system, from patient outcomes to plan costs to quality ratings.

Pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs), pharmacies, and providers already know nonadherence matters. What they need to work out is how to find and close the gaps behind it before those gaps cause avoidable harm.

## The Clinical and Financial Stakes

The numbers are large. Studies have estimated that nonadherence contributes to approximately 125,000 deaths a year in the United States and accounts for up to 10% of hospitalizations. Avoidable healthcare spending tied to nonadherence has been estimated at $100 billion to $300 billion annually.

Behind these figures are patients whose health gets worse. Patients who skip antihypertensives face a higher risk of stroke and heart attack. Patients who stop taking statins lose their cardiovascular protection. Diabetic patients who miss doses of oral agents or insulin see their glycemic control slip and their risk of complications rise. Every missed refill can lead to a later event that could have been prevented and that costs money.

## Cost: A Leading Barrier to Adherence

Adherence depends on many factors, including side effects, complex regimens, health literacy, and forgetfulness, but cost consistently ranks near the top. Research has shown that prescription abandonment climbs steeply as patients' out-of-pocket costs go up. A patient who reaches the pharmacy counter and sees a copay they cannot afford often leaves the prescription unfilled.

Brand-name medications show this most clearly. Under high-deductible health plans and coinsurance, patients pay a larger share of the list price, and many of them leave without the drug.

### The Pharmacist's Role in Steering Toward Generics

Pharmacists are well placed to step in at that moment. When a patient abandons or delays a brand-name prescription, the pharmacist can work with the prescriber to recommend a therapeutically equivalent or clinically appropriate lower-cost alternative, often a generic with established efficacy and safety.

The evidence backs this up. Patients started on generic medications show higher adherence than those started on brand-name equivalents, mostly because they pay less. If pharmacists can see unfilled claims and workable alternatives in real time, they can turn a lost prescription into a therapy that actually starts.

## Accountability Is Increasing

The pressure to improve adherence is also regulatory. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) gives medication adherence measures heavy weight in the Medicare Part D Star Ratings, specifically for diabetes medications, renin-angiotensin system (RAS) antagonists, and statins. Because these measures are triple-weighted, they move overall plan ratings substantially, and those ratings affect bonus payments and member enrollment.

CMS keeps refining its quality programs, adjusting measure methodology and putting more emphasis on health equity, so plans and their partners will be held more accountable for adherence results. Organizations that respond only after the fact, looking back at past data, will fall further behind.

## Moving Beyond One-Size-Fits-All Programs

Many traditional adherence programs apply the same interventions, such as automated refill reminders or generic outreach calls, across large populations. These tactics have a role, but they often miss the particular reasons a given patient stops taking a medication.

Segmenting patients by risk and need works better. Take the newly diagnosed patient. Research indicates that a substantial share of patients who start a new chronic medication stop within the first year, and many stop after the first fill. They may not yet understand their condition, may have early side effects, or may doubt the medication is worth taking. Targeted early outreach, including pharmacist counseling at initiation and follow-up within the first 30 days, can greatly improve long-term persistence.

Other segments need their own strategies: patients on complex polypharmacy regimens, patients with cost-sensitive benefit designs, people with behavioral health conditions, and members facing social determinants of health such as transportation barriers. Targeting each group precisely puts resources where they will do the most good.

## Digital Therapeutics and Compliance Packaging

New tools are giving organizations more ways to address nonadherence.

**Digital therapeutics and connected tools** include smartphone apps, smart pill bottles, and remote monitoring platforms that remind patients to take doses, track behavior, and notify care teams when medications are missed. Some evidence-based digital therapeutics have received FDA clearance and provide structured behavioral interventions alongside pharmacotherapy.

**Compliance packaging**, such as blister packs and multi-dose adherence packaging organized by date and time, makes complex regimens easier to follow, especially for elderly patients and people managing several chronic conditions. Studies have shown that this packaging, combined with medication synchronization programs, can meaningfully raise proportion of days covered (PDC).

These tools work best when they go to the patients most likely to benefit, not to everyone.

## Proactive, Population-Level Analytics: Intervening Before Nonadherence Occurs

What these strategies share is a dependence on timing. Adherence programs too often act after a gap has opened: after a refill has lapsed, after a PDC threshold has been missed, or after a patient has been hospitalized.

Proactive, population-level analytics make earlier action possible. Advanced analytics platforms combine pharmacy claims, medical data, benefit design, and patient-level risk factors to predict which members are most likely to become nonadherent, and why, before it happens. Predictive models can flag newly diagnosed patients at risk of stopping early, point out members whose rising out-of-pocket costs put continuity at risk, and identify chances for generic substitution or benefit adjustments.

Using these insights, PBMs, pharmacies, and providers can coordinate targeted interventions, spend resources efficiently, and show measurable gains in both outcomes and quality ratings.

Medication nonadherence can be solved. It consists of specific gaps that can be identified, and with the right data and the right partners, those gaps can be closed one patient segment at a time.
