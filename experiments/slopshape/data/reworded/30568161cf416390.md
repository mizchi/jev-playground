# How to Write a Job Description That Attracts the Right Candidates (and Filters Out the Wrong Ones)

When unemployment is high, a single job posting can bring in hundreds of applications within days, and you end up spending hours reading resumes that are nowhere near what you need.

A well-written job description is one of the most effective ways to control that volume. It draws qualified candidates, lets poor fits rule themselves out, and saves time at every stage of hiring. Here's how to write one.

## Start Before You Write

Before drafting anything, pin down what the role requires. Define:

- **Responsibilities:** What will this person do each day, week, and month?
- **Success measures:** What does great performance look like after 30, 90, and 180 days?
- **Skills:** Which technical and soft skills are essential to doing the job well?
- **Culture fit:** In what kind of work environment will this person do best? Fast-paced and independent? Collaborative and structured?

Speak with the people who will work alongside this hire. What they tell you will help you describe the role accurately and prevent surprises later.

## Weigh Potential Against Experience

Asking for years of experience in the identical role is tempting, but experience doesn't always predict success best. Candidates with strong transferable skills, a history of learning quickly, and real enthusiasm often outperform people whose main qualification is the required number of years.

Hiring for potential also enlarges your talent pool. Rigid experience requirements can unintentionally exclude career changers, returning parents, veterans, and people from underrepresented backgrounds who haven't had equal access to traditional career paths. Considering potential can bring fresh perspectives and meaningful diversity to your team.

## Separate Must-Haves from Nice-to-Haves

A long requirements list can put off strong candidates, especially those who rarely apply unless they meet every qualification. List only what is truly essential as essential.

Divide your qualifications into two sections:

- **Must-haves:** The non-negotiable skills, certifications, or experience the job requires.
- **Nice-to-haves:** Extra qualifications that would help but can be learned on the job.

With this split, qualified candidates can apply confidently, and unqualified candidates can see that the role doesn't suit them.

## Sell the Company and the Role

Even in a competitive job market, the best candidates have choices. A job description has to market the job, not just list requirements.

Explain why candidates would want to work for you. Describe your mission, your team, growth opportunities, benefits, flexibility, and what sets your culture apart. Show how this role fits into the company's larger goals. Candidates who can picture themselves succeeding in the position are the ones who apply out of real interest rather than because they'd take any job.

## Watch for Gendered Language

Wording affects who applies. Some terms quietly suggest that a role suits one gender more than another. Words such as "rockstar," "ninja," "aggressive," or "dominant" may discourage some candidates, and overly soft language can do the reverse.

Use neutral, inclusive wording. Address the candidate directly as "you," and describe the work instead of personality stereotypes. Free online tools can check your job description for gender-coded language and suggest alternatives.

## Keep It Concise and Well-Structured

Candidates skim, so use a clear structure that helps them find what they need:

1. **Job title:** Clear and searchable. Skip the creative titles.
2. **Summary:** A few sentences about the role and why it matters.
3. **Responsibilities:** A focused bullet list of key duties.
4. **Qualifications:** Must-haves and nice-to-haves.
5. **About the company:** Your mission, culture, and benefits.
6. **How to apply:** Clear next steps.

Write short paragraphs and remove jargon. A tight description is easier to read and more likely to draw serious applicants.

## Gather Feedback and Proofread

Before posting, show the draft to a few colleagues you trust, ideally including someone who currently holds a similar role. Ask whether it describes the job accurately, whether it would interest them, and whether anything is confusing or missing.

Then proofread carefully. Typos and errors make a company look careless and can deter detail-oriented candidates.

## Add Pre-Screening Questions

If you expect many applicants, pre-screening questions save a great deal of time. Add a few short questions to the application that cover your must-haves, such as:

- "Do you have a valid certification in X?"
- "Are you able to work on-site three days per week?"
- "Describe a time you managed a project from start to finish."

The answers let you spot qualified candidates quickly and screen out those who lack key requirements, so your time goes to the most promising applicants.
