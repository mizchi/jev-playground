# Enhanced Due Diligence Isn't the Exception. It's Part of the Job.

Many compliance teams handle enhanced due diligence (EDD) as an emergency measure, kept for the rare customer who is obviously suspicious. That view is outdated, and it carries risk. Under current regulation, EDD is a routine, predictable obligation that financial institutions must apply whenever a customer's risk profile requires it.

A team that already runs customer due diligence (CDD) has the groundwork in place. You will need EDD; what matters is whether you know exactly when to increase scrutiny and whether you can do so consistently, efficiently, and in a way regulators accept.

## The KYC Regulatory Backdrop

In the United States, Know Your Customer (KYC) requirements come from the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) and are reinforced by the USA PATRIOT Act. Under these laws, financial institutions must verify customer identities, understand the nature of customer relationships, and detect and report suspicious activity.

The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) made these expectations more specific in its 2016 Customer Due Diligence Final Rule, which in effect set out four core elements of CDD:

1. Identifying and verifying the identity of customers
2. Identifying and verifying the identity of beneficial owners of legal entity customers
3. Understanding the nature and purpose of customer relationships to develop a customer risk profile
4. Conducting ongoing monitoring to identify and report suspicious transactions and, on a risk basis, maintain and update customer information

EDD extends this baseline. For higher-risk customers, institutions are expected to collect more information, verify it more strictly, and watch the relationship more closely. The Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council (FFIEC) BSA/AML Examination Manual offers a practical framework for spotting higher risk and sorts it into three categories: customers and entities, geographic locations, and products and services.

## Customers and Entities

Some types of customers carry a greater built-in risk of money laundering, corruption, or terrorist financing.

### Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs)

PEPs are individuals who hold or have held prominent public positions, together with their family members and close associates. They present elevated risk because they may have access to public funds and influence. U.S. regulations specifically address senior foreign political figures, particularly with respect to private banking accounts. EDD for PEPs usually involves verifying source of wealth and source of funds, getting senior management approval, and reviewing the relationship more often.

### Beneficial Ownership and Complex Structures

Shell companies, trusts, and layered corporate structures can hide who really controls an account. FinCEN's CDD Rule requires institutions to identify every individual who owns 25% or more of a legal entity, plus at least one individual with significant control. When ownership is unclear, crosses several jurisdictions, or involves nominee shareholders, EDD is needed to determine the true beneficiary.

### Cash-Intensive Businesses

Businesses that take in large amounts of cash, such as convenience stores, restaurants, car washes, parking garages, and money services businesses, can be used to mix illicit funds with legitimate revenue. EDD for these customers concentrates on learning their expected transaction patterns and confirming that actual activity matches the business's stated operations.

## Geographic Locations

Risk also depends on where a customer lives, operates, or transacts.

### FATF-Monitored Jurisdictions

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) publishes lists of jurisdictions with strategic AML deficiencies, both those subject to a call for action (the "blacklist") and those under increased monitoring (the "grey list"). Relationships and transactions involving these jurisdictions need closer scrutiny, and FinCEN regularly issues advisories that reinforce FATF's findings.

### Sanctioned Countries and Parties

The Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) runs U.S. sanctions programs aimed at countries, entities, and individuals. Sanctions screening carries strict liability: a transaction with a sanctioned party can bring significant penalties whatever the intent. Customers linked to comprehensively sanctioned regions, or to parties on the Specially Designated Nationals (SDN) list, require an immediate and thorough review.

### High-Risk Financial Centers

Jurisdictions known for secrecy, weak regulatory oversight, or high levels of corruption and narcotics trafficking also call for enhanced attention, and that includes the domestically designated High Intensity Financial Crime Areas (HIFCAs).

## Products and Services

Some products are easier to exploit for illicit purposes than others.

### Correspondent Banking

Through correspondent accounts, foreign banks gain access to the U.S. financial system, often on behalf of their own customers, whom the U.S. institution never sees. Section 312 of the USA PATRIOT Act requires EDD on correspondent accounts held for certain foreign banks, which includes assessing the foreign bank's AML controls, its ownership, and the jurisdictions where it operates.

### Other Higher-Risk Offerings

Private banking, wire transfers, trade finance, remote deposit capture, prepaid access, and virtual asset services can all move funds quickly, across borders, or anonymously. EDD should follow when customers use these products, particularly alongside other risk factors.

## What Regulators Expect

Examiners do not expect every customer to be treated as high risk. They expect a **risk-based approach**: a documented, defensible method for assessing customer risk and applying controls in proportion to it.

In practice, that requires:

- **Clear risk-rating criteria.** Your program should spell out which factors make a customer high risk and trigger EDD.
- **Thorough documentation.** Record every EDD decision, including the information collected, the analysis done, and the approvals received. Examiners will assume that anything undocumented never happened.
- **Ongoing monitoring.** EDD does not end at onboarding. Review high-risk customers on a regular schedule and whenever their activity or circumstances change, for example a new PEP designation, a change in ownership, or an updated sanctions list.
- **Consistency.** Treat similar customers alike, and make sure your process gives repeatable results.

## Automation Makes Routine EDD Practical

A routine obligation needs routine infrastructure. Manual reviews have trouble keeping up with sanctions lists, PEP databases, and FATF designations that change constantly, and they bring inconsistency and human error.

With automated identity verification and AML watchlist screening, compliance teams can check customers against global sanctions, PEP, and adverse media sources in real time, flag high-risk triggers as soon as they appear, and keep monitoring existing customers as the lists change. Automated tools also leave a clear audit trail, which supports the documentation regulators expect.

Enhanced due diligence is a core, recurring part of a sound compliance program, not a rare escalation. Institutions that approach it this way and give their teams the right tools are best placed to satisfy regulators and keep financial crime out of their systems.
