# What Are Coding Bootcamps? (Part 1 of 2)

In the past decade, coding bootcamps have grown from a new experiment into an established route into the tech industry. Their pitch can sound implausible: learn to code in a few months and get a well-paid job as a software developer. Thousands of students have done exactly that.

What are coding bootcamps, though, where did they come from, and how do they compare with a traditional computer science degree? This first part of our two-part series covers where the bootcamp model came from, how programs work, who enrolls, and what the data shows about cost and outcomes. Part 2 will examine criticisms of bootcamps and where the model may go next.

## The Origins of Coding Bootcamps

Coding bootcamps appeared in the early 2010s to address a specific problem: the tech industry couldn't hire enough qualified developers. As businesses of all kinds moved online and startups multiplied, demand for software engineers soared. Universities weren't turning out computer science graduates quickly enough to meet it, and many graduates lacked the practical, job-ready skills employers were looking for.

Entrepreneurs spotted an opening. Could people learn the specific skills an entry-level developer job requires in a fraction of the time a degree takes?

**Dev Bootcamp**, founded in San Francisco in 2012, is widely considered one of the first coding bootcamps. Its intensive, immersive web development program drew attention for its ambition and strong placement results. Other pioneers, including **App Academy**, **Hack Reactor**, **General Assembly**, and **The Flatiron School**, started programs around the same time.

Several early bootcamps tried business models tied directly to job placement. App Academy was well known for charging no tuition up front; students instead paid a percentage of their first-year salary once they were hired. Other bootcamps collected referral fees from employers that hired their graduates. Because the schools made money only when students succeeded, these models helped a new and untested kind of education earn credibility.

## How Coding Bootcamps Work

### Formats and Delivery Modes

Bootcamps offer several formats to suit different schedules and ways of learning:

- **Full-time immersive programs** usually last 12 to 16 weeks and demand 40 to 60 or more hours a week.
- **Part-time programs** run six months to a year, so students can keep their jobs and study in the evenings and on weekends.
- **In-person programs** meet on physical campuses, often in major tech hubs.
- **Online programs**, which have expanded rapidly in recent years, provide live or self-paced instruction that students can take from anywhere.

### Subject Coverage

Early bootcamps taught web development almost exclusively. Programs today cover a much wider range, including:

- Full-stack web development
- Front-end and back-end development
- Data science and analytics
- Cybersecurity
- UX/UI design
- Mobile app development
- Product management

Bootcamps center on practical, project-based learning. Students usually assemble a portfolio of real applications to show employers, and many programs offer career services such as resume reviews, mock interviews, and networking events.

### University Partnerships

As the industry has matured, many universities have teamed up with bootcamp providers to run programs under the university's name. The university reaches new audiences with practical, career-focused training, and the bootcamp company borrows an established institution's credibility. Graduates may receive a university certificate, but these programs generally don't award academic credit.

## Who Attends Coding Bootcamps?

Bootcamp students have many different backgrounds, but **career changers** make up a prominent group.

Many students already have a bachelor's degree, often in a field unrelated to technology, such as the humanities, social sciences, or business. Some found that their degree didn't bring the job or salary they wanted; others have spent years in careers they no longer find fulfilling. A bootcamp gives them a fairly quick, focused way into a growing, well-paid field without four more years of school.

Bootcamps also draw recent graduates who want practical skills on top of their education, professionals upskilling for their current jobs, and people without college degrees looking for an accessible way into tech.

## Bootcamps vs. Computer Science Degrees

How does a bootcamp compare with a traditional four-year computer science degree? Published industry research provides some useful points of comparison.

### Cost

Industry research from Course Report puts the average full-time coding bootcamp at roughly $13,000 to $14,000, with prices from a few thousand dollars to more than $20,000. A four-year degree, by contrast, can cost tens of thousands of dollars at a public in-state university and well over $200,000 at a private one, not counting living expenses and lost income.

### Duration

Most full-time bootcamps take about three to four months, while a computer science degree usually takes four years. For career changers who must keep earning, that gap matters a great deal.

### Salary Outcomes

Course Report's research found that bootcamp graduates reported an average starting salary of about $65,000 to $70,000, roughly 50% more on average than they earned before the bootcamp. Computer science graduates generally start higher, often at $70,000 to $80,000 or more depending on the source and region, and may qualify for a wider range of roles, especially in specialized or research-heavy areas.

The two groups aren't directly comparable, however. Bootcamp students often already have degrees and may bring professional experience from other fields, whereas CS graduates are often entering the workforce for the first time.

### Depth of Learning

Bootcamps do provide less depth than a traditional computer science education. A CS degree covers fundamentals such as algorithms, data structures, computer architecture, operating systems, and mathematical theory in much more detail, and that grounding can matter for some roles and for long-term career growth. Bootcamps deliberately concentrate on the practical skills needed to get hired and become productive fast, so graduates often have to fill theoretical gaps by continuing to learn on the job.

## Looking Ahead

Coding bootcamps have earned a real place in education. They provide a faster, cheaper, and highly practical path into tech, especially for career changers seeking a new start, and many students describe them as a life-changing investment.

The model has its problems, though. Several early pioneers, Dev Bootcamp among them, have since shut down, and the industry has faced ongoing questions about outcomes reporting, quality, and value.
