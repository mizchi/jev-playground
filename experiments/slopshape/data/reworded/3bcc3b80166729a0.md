## What Is an EKG/ECG?

An electrocardiogram is a test that records the heart's electrical activity. EKG and ECG are the same test. "ECG" follows the English spelling, *electrocardiogram*, and "EKG" follows the German *Elektrokardiogramm*. In the United States the two terms are used interchangeably, and many clinicians favor "EKG" so it isn't confused with "EEG," a test of brain activity.

### How an EKG Works

An electrical signal sets off every heartbeat. It begins in the sinoatrial (SA) node, a small cluster of cells in the heart's upper right chamber that is often called the heart's natural pacemaker. The impulse moves through the upper chambers (the atria) and makes them contract. It then passes through the atrioventricular (AV) node into the lower chambers (the ventricles), which contract and pump blood to the lungs and the rest of the body.

An EKG machine picks up these very small electrical signals through electrodes attached to the skin of the chest, arms, and legs. A standard resting EKG uses 10 electrodes to generate 12 "leads," each showing the heart's electrical activity from a different angle.

The machine amplifies the signals and records them as a waveform on paper or on a screen. Each segment of the waveform matches a specific event in the cardiac cycle:

- **P wave:** The electrical activation of the atria.
- **QRS complex:** The rapid activation of the ventricles.
- **T wave:** The ventricles recovering, or "resetting," before the next beat.

From the shape, size, timing, and rhythm of these waves, clinicians can learn a lot about how the heart is working. The test is painless, takes just a few minutes, and uses no radiation.

### What Conditions Can an EKG Help Diagnose?

EKGs help detect and monitor many heart conditions, including:

- **Arrhythmias:** Irregular heart rhythms, such as atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, or heart rates that are too fast (tachycardia) or too slow (bradycardia).
- **Heart attacks:** Both heart attacks in progress (myocardial infarctions) and evidence of earlier ones.
- **Coronary artery disease:** Signs of reduced blood flow to the heart muscle, called ischemia.
- **Conduction abnormalities:** Problems in how electrical signals move through the heart, such as bundle branch blocks or heart block.
- **Enlarged heart chambers:** Thickened heart walls or enlarged chambers, which can indicate high blood pressure or other conditions.
- **Electrolyte imbalances:** Abnormal potassium, calcium, or magnesium levels can alter the EKG pattern.
- **Effects of medications:** Some drugs change the heart's electrical activity, and EKGs let clinicians watch for these effects.
- **Pacemaker function:** Confirming that an implanted device is working correctly.

### Where Are EKG Machines Used?

EKG machines are fast, portable, and highly informative, so they appear throughout healthcare:

- **Emergency departments:** For rapid evaluation of chest pain, shortness of breath, and other urgent symptoms.
- **Hospitals:** In inpatient units, intensive care units, cardiac care units, and preoperative assessment areas.
- **Cardiology practices:** For diagnosis, ongoing monitoring, and stress testing.
- **Primary care clinics:** During routine checkups and screenings.
- **Urgent care centers:** To help triage patients with cardiac symptoms.
- **Ambulatory surgery centers:** For preoperative screening.
- **Occupational health and sports medicine:** For pre-employment physicals and athlete screenings.

Facilities that run a high volume of EKGs need a reliable machine with a strong feature set.

## Refurbished vs. Used: What's the Difference?

Shoppers for pre-owned medical equipment often see both "refurbished" and "used." The terms sound alike but mean different things.

**Used equipment** is usually sold as is. It may have been taken out of service at another facility and resold without testing, repair, or cleaning. Its service history, whether it works fully, and whether it meets manufacturer specifications may all be unknown. A used purchase can bring hidden problems and unplanned repair bills later.

**Refurbished equipment** has been thoroughly inspected, tested, repaired, and restored to the original equipment manufacturer's (OEM) specifications. It is cleaned, calibrated, and checked to confirm it performs as intended. Refurbished equipment, in other words, is ready for clinical use.
