# Your Birth Control Deserves More Than a Quiz

Maybe you've spent months or years moving from pills to patches to rings to IUDs, looking for one that doesn't give you headaches, mood swings, weight changes, or breakthrough bleeding. If so, you've already seen that the way we choose contraception doesn't work.

For millions of people, finding the right birth control means trial and error. You start a method and wait three months to see whether the side effects settle. If they don't, you return to your provider, switch methods, and restart the three-month wait. Some people repeat this for years. Others stop using contraception entirely.

Online birth control quizzes have sprung up to answer that frustration, promising to match you with the right method in a few clicks. The idea has obvious appeal. But when we at adyn reviewed some of the most popular quizzes, we found that they don't give people what they need, and that they point to a larger problem in how contraceptive care is delivered.

## Quizzes Are a Symptom, Not a Solution

Birth control quizzes exist because the standard of care leaves people to search for answers themselves.

Contraceptive prescribing today depends mostly on provider habit and convenience. A clinician may reach for the pill they prescribe most, the one with samples in the cabinet, or the one they know best. Appointments are short, with counseling sometimes lasting only a few minutes, and there is seldom time to dig into a patient's full history, preferences, and biology.

Individual providers are often working under tight constraints, so the fault lies with the system. When it doesn't offer personalization, people look for it elsewhere. Online quizzes supply something that looks like personalization but lacks its substance.

## What We Found When We Reviewed Popular Birth Control Quizzes

We examined widely used birth control quizzes from health websites, brands, and advocacy organizations. Answering as several different hypothetical users, we compared the questions each quiz asked and the methods it recommended. Five shortcomings stood out.

### 1. The Questions Are Inconsistent

No standard defines what a birth control quiz should ask. Some quizzes focused almost entirely on lifestyle preferences, such as whether you'd remember to take a daily pill. Others centered on future pregnancy plans. A few asked which side effects you want to avoid; others never mentioned side effects.

As a result, one person can take three quizzes and get three different recommendations. When a tool's answer depends more on who built it than on who is using it, it isn't personalizing anything.

### 2. The Prompts Are Confusing

Many quizzes used vague or clinical terms without defining them. A question like "Do you want a method that affects your cycle?" means different things to different people: lighter periods, no periods, or more predictable timing?

Other questions assumed medical knowledge many users lack, for example asking whether someone prefers estrogen-containing methods without explaining what that means or why it matters. Confusing questions produce unreliable answers, which in turn produce unreliable recommendations.

### 3. They Don't Actually Narrow the Options

The most surprising finding was that many quizzes recommended nearly every method. After a dozen questions, users often saw a long list of "good matches" covering pills, patches, rings, shots, implants, and IUDs.

A list like that is a brochure, not guidance. People take quizzes to get help with a decision, and a roster of almost every option on the market leaves them no closer to making one.

### 4. They Omit Critical Clotting Risk

This shortcoming worries us most. Estrogen-containing contraceptives, including many combined pills, the patch, and the ring, are linked to a higher risk of venous thromboembolism, or blood clots. The risk is low for most people. For some, though, it is much higher because of personal or family history, smoking, age, migraines with aura, or inherited clotting conditions such as Factor V Leiden.

Many of the quizzes we reviewed never asked about clotting history, and some skipped smoking or migraines as well. A quiz like that could point a high-risk person toward a method that is genuinely dangerous for them. Any tool that claims to recommend birth control should, at the very least, screen for the factors that determine whether estrogen is safe.

### 5. They Can't Account for Individual Biology

Even a well-designed quiz is limited to what you can report about yourself. It has no view of how your body metabolizes hormones, and it can't detect genetic variants that may shape your response to a given method or your risk of certain side effects.

Two people who give identical answers can have very different experiences on the same pill. That difference comes from biology, which multiple-choice questions can't capture.

## What You Can Do Instead

You don't have to rely on guesswork. These practical steps can help you get more from the process.

### Prepare for Your Counseling Appointment

Contraceptive counseling appointments tend to be brief, so arrive prepared. Before your visit:

- **Write down your history.** List the methods you've tried, how long you used each, and the side effects you had.
- **Know your family medical history.** Above all, find out whether any close relatives have had blood clots, strokes, or clotting disorders.
- **Clarify your priorities.** Which matters most: avoiding weight gain, mood stability, lighter periods, or not having to think about it every day? Put them in order.
- **Be upfront about your lifestyle.** Smoking, migraines, and other health conditions all affect which methods are safe for you.
- **Ask direct questions.** Why is this method recommended for me in particular? What would we try next if it doesn't work? Which side effects should I watch for?

Preparation gives your provider more to base a recommendation on, and it shows that you expect a real conversation rather than a default prescription.

### Seek Out Precision-Medicine Tools

In fields like oncology and cardiology, precision medicine has changed care by using a patient's biology to guide treatment. Contraception has no reason to lag behind.

Tools such as adyn's Birth Control Test combine genetic and hormonal insights with your health history to give personalized information about how you may respond to different methods, including risk factors like clotting that quizzes routinely overlook. You and your provider can begin with data about your body instead of a provider's habit or a generic list.

The test doesn't replace the conversation with your clinician; it makes that conversation far more productive.

## The Bigger Picture: Women's Health Deserves Investment

Birth control has been widely available for more than 60 years, yet the way people choose a method has hardly changed. Tens of millions of people use contraception, and a large share of them will stop or switch methods because of side effects. Each switch costs time and discomfort, and some lead to unintended pregnancies.

The popularity of online quizzes shows that people want better tools. They want personalization and want their own experience to count, and they will go looking for it themselves when the system doesn't provide it.

Meeting that demand takes investment in research, diagnostics, and innovation that give women's health the rigor it deserves. Women's health has long been underfunded and understudied, and conditions that mainly affect women receive a fraction of the research dollars spent in other areas. Contraception is one of the clearest everyday examples of that neglect.

We believe choosing a birth control method should be as precise as any other medical decision. The outcome shouldn't hinge on which quiz you happened to find or which pill your provider prescribes most. It should hinge on you.

A quiz can spark curiosity. For your health, though, you deserve more than a quiz: a standard of care built around your biology.
