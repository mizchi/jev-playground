# Biologics vs. Biosimilars: What Plan Sponsors Need to Know About the Next Wave of Drug Savings

Specialty drugs make up a small fraction of prescriptions but a large, expanding portion of pharmacy spend. Many plan sponsors now find that a few high-cost biologic medications account for much of their total drug costs. Meanwhile, patents are running out on some of the top-selling biologics ever made, which clears the way for cheaper alternatives called biosimilars.

Benefits decision-makers who want to hold down costs without hurting patient care need to understand how biologics and biosimilars differ, and how both differ from traditional generics. This post lays out those differences.

## What Are Biologics?

Biologics are medications derived from living organisms such as cells, yeast, or bacteria. Most traditional drugs are small molecules made by chemical synthesis; biologics are large, complex molecules made through biological processes. Monoclonal antibodies, vaccines, insulin, and therapeutic proteins are all biologics.

Their complexity lets biologics treat many serious and chronic conditions, among them rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's disease, psoriasis, multiple sclerosis, and many forms of cancer. For millions of patients, they've changed what treatment looks like.

The same complexity drives up their price. Making biologics takes sophisticated manufacturing, careful handling, and lengthy clinical development. Many run tens of thousands of dollars per patient per year, and they're among the fastest-growing categories of pharmacy spend.

## What Are Biosimilars?

A biosimilar is a biologic that closely resembles a biologic the FDA has already approved, called the reference product. For approval, the biosimilar's manufacturer has to show that the two products have no clinically meaningful differences in safety, purity, or potency.

The Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act (BPCIA) of 2010 set up an abbreviated approval pathway for biosimilars. Under it, manufacturers can lean partly on the reference product's existing data, which cuts development time and cost. What comes out is a product that works the same way as the original biologic and treats the same conditions, but costs less.

## How Biosimilars Differ From Generics

People often call biosimilars "generic biologics," but that label is misleading.

A generic drug is an exact chemical copy of a small-molecule brand-name drug. Small molecules can be replicated precisely, so the generic has the same active ingredient as the brand. Getting a generic approved mostly comes down to showing bioequivalence.

Biologics are made in living systems, so even the original manufacturer's product varies slightly between batches. No one can make an identical copy of a biologic. Biosimilar makers instead have to prove their product is highly similar to the reference product, differing only in minor ways in clinically inactive components.

That difference affects cost. Developing a biosimilar is harder than developing a generic, and it usually costs more and takes longer. Biosimilars therefore tend to come with smaller discounts. Generics can sell for 80 to 90 percent less than the brand, while biosimilars have historically launched at discounts of about 15 to 35 percent, although some recent launches have been priced far lower.

## What Interchangeability Means for Pharmacy Substitution

A pharmacist can't swap every biosimilar in for its reference product. The FDA grants a separate designation for that, called **interchangeability**.

To be designated interchangeable, a biosimilar has to meet further requirements showing it can be expected to produce the same clinical result as the reference product in any given patient, and that switching back and forth between them doesn't raise risk or lower effectiveness. With that designation, a pharmacist may substitute the biosimilar for the reference product without involving the prescriber, subject to state pharmacy laws.

Interchangeability matters to plan sponsors because it simplifies substitution and can speed adoption. Without it, switching members to a biosimilar usually means getting a new prescription, which can slow uptake and cap savings.

## Humira: The Biosimilar Test Case

Humira (adalimumab) shows what's at stake more clearly than any other drug. It was the world's best-selling drug for years, bringing in roughly $20 billion in annual global revenue at its peak. It treats a range of autoimmune conditions, and its list price rose to more than $6,000 per month.

Europe had Humira biosimilars years before the U.S. did; here, patent settlements held off competition until 2023. Amgen's Amjevita launched first, in January 2023. In July 2023, a wave of other adalimumab biosimilars followed, including Cyltezo, Hyrimoz, Hadlima, Yusimry, Hulio, Idacio, and Yuflyma. Boehringer Ingelheim's Cyltezo was the first biosimilar to be designated interchangeable with Humira, and others have since received the same designation.

Several of these products came to market at two price points: a high list price paired with bigger rebates, and a low list price, in some cases more than 80 percent below Humira's. For plan sponsors, that structure brings both opportunity and complexity. The net savings from switching from Humira to a biosimilar can vary widely depending on formulary strategy, rebate arrangements, and PBM contracts.

Adoption started slowly because many PBMs at first kept Humira on formulary next to the biosimilars. Once major PBMs started dropping Humira from their national formularies in favor of biosimilars, market share shifted quickly. The lesson for plan sponsors is plain: biosimilar savings are real, but you only capture them if you understand the market and manage formulary decisions actively.

## A Growing Market

More biologics are following Humira. Stelara (ustekinumab) already faces biosimilar competition, and other blockbuster products will lose patent protection over the next few years. Analysts project that the global biosimilars market could expand from about $30 billion in 2023 to well over $70 billion by the end of the decade, and some estimates put it above $100 billion.

Biosimilars could cut U.S. drug spending by tens of billions of dollars over the next several years. That makes them one of the largest cost-management opportunities in specialty pharmacy for employers and health plans.

## What This Means for Plan Sponsors

Benefits decision-makers can get the most out of biosimilars if they:

- **Monitor patent expirations** and upcoming biosimilar launches for drugs their population uses.
- **Understand net cost**, not only list price, by factoring in rebates and pricing structures.
- **Review PBM formulary strategy** to make sure biosimilars are set up to deliver savings.
- **Consider member experience**, including communication and support when members switch therapies.
- **Forecast the impact** biosimilar adoption will have on future drug spend.
