# Why a Recession Won't Hand Hiring Power Back to Employers, and How HR Teams Should Adapt

Whenever economists hint at a recession, boardrooms and HR departments return to a familiar assumption: power is about to shift back to employers. The reasoning is that a slowing economy makes jobs scarce and candidates desperate, so companies can dictate the terms of employment again.

Acting on that assumption could be expensive.

Economic uncertainty, rising interest rates, and news of layoffs in some sectors have not broken the labor market, which has held up remarkably well. Unemployment remains low by historical standards, and job openings still outnumber available workers in many industries. Most important, workers now expect different things. The pandemic changed how people think about work, where they do it, and what they will tolerate from an employer.

A rough economy, then, won't end the candidate-driven market. HR professionals and talent acquisition leaders who wait for their leverage to return may lose top talent to competitors that adapted sooner.

Below, we explain why the old playbook no longer works and what HR teams should do now.

## The New Reality: Workers Have Changed

In past downturns, employers learned to expect a surge of applicants and employees grateful just to be employed. Today's workers think differently.

Surveys over the past several years have consistently found employees putting more weight on flexibility, well-being, purpose, and growth. Many workers say they would consider leaving for a job with more flexibility even if it paid no more. A significant share say they would turn down a job that required full-time office attendance. Younger generations in particular increasingly want to work for organizations that share their values.

Structural factors matter as well. Demographic shifts, including baby boomer retirements and lower birth rates, mean fewer workers are entering the labor force in many developed economies. Skills gaps in technology, healthcare, and skilled trades persist in any economy, so companies will keep competing hard for talent in those fields even during a slowdown.

A recession may slow hiring in some industries, but workers' expectations will remain. Employers that overlook this risk higher turnover, weaker employer brands, and longer time-to-fill for critical roles.

## Strategy 1: Track Economic and Labor Market Data

Adapting your recruitment strategy starts with understanding the conditions you're working in. Too many HR teams rely on assumptions or headlines instead of hard data.

Monitor key indicators regularly, including:

- **Unemployment rates**, nationally and within your industry and region
- **Job openings and quits data**, which show how confident workers are about finding new jobs
- **Wage growth trends**, which help you benchmark your compensation packages
- **Industry-specific hiring and layoff activity**, which can signal changes in talent availability

With this data, your decisions can be informed rather than reactive. If tech sector layoffs free up experienced professionals, for example, your team can reach out to them quickly. If quit rates stay high in your industry, you'll know retention deserves top priority.

Examine your own numbers as well: time-to-hire, cost-per-hire, offer acceptance rates, the points where candidates drop off, and the sources of your best hires. Internal and external data together show much more accurately where you stand and where to concentrate.

## Strategy 2: Promote Upskilling and Reskilling Programs

When talent is scarce, the best candidate may already work for you. Upskilling and reskilling programs let you close skill gaps internally and show current and prospective employees that you are investing in their growth.

Career development consistently ranks among the top factors employees weigh when deciding whether to join or stay with a company. Research has repeatedly found that a large majority of employees say they would stay longer at a company that invested in their learning and development.

Consider programs that include:

- **Structured learning paths** tied to career progression
- **Mentorship and coaching** from senior team members
- **Tuition reimbursement or learning stipends** for courses and certifications
- **Internal mobility programs** that make moving into new roles easy

Advertise these opportunities in your job postings and employer branding. Candidates want to know both what the job involves today and where it could lead.

## Strategy 3: Take Authentic Stances on Social Issues

Workers today, especially millennials and Gen Z, increasingly expect employers to stand for something. Surveys show that many job seekers weigh a company's position on social issues, such as diversity, equity and inclusion, environmental sustainability, and community involvement, when evaluating employers.

What matters is authenticity. Candidates quickly recognize empty gestures and performative statements, so any public stance your organization takes must be backed by meaningful action.

You can show genuine commitment by:

- **Publishing measurable DEI goals** and reporting on progress
- **Supporting employee resource groups** with real resources and executive sponsorship
- **Committing to sustainability initiatives** with transparent reporting
- **Encouraging volunteer work** through paid volunteer days or community partnerships
- **Ensuring pay equity** through regular audits

When your values match those of your employees and candidates, you gain an advantage in attracting and keeping talent that competitors can't easily copy.

## Strategy 4: Invest in Candidate Engagement Technology

In a competitive market, candidate experience can decide whether you land a hire. Top candidates often have several options and won't wait weeks to hear back. A slow, confusing, or impersonal process can push them to a competitor.

Technology is central to a smooth candidate experience. Modern recruitment tools can help you:

- **Respond to applicants quickly** with automated acknowledgments and updates
- **Streamline scheduling** so interviews don't stall in long email chains
- **Personalize communication** at scale
- **Identify the best-fit candidates faster** with AI-powered matching and screening
- **Keep your talent pipeline warm** so you can reengage past candidates when new roles open

An applicant tracking system (ATS) combines these capabilities, helping your team stay organized, collaborate better, and move candidates through the pipeline efficiently. When hiring volume rises or falls with the economy, the right technology lets you scale without losing quality.

## Strategy 5: Offer Whole-Person Wellness Benefits

The pandemic put mental health and overall well-being at the center of workplace discussions, and those concerns persist. Burnout is still widespread, and employees increasingly expect employers to support them as whole people, not only as workers.

Surveys have found that a majority of employees would consider leaving a job that doesn't support their mental health, and that well-being benefits carry growing weight in job decisions.

Whole-person wellness benefits might include:

- **Mental health support**, such as counseling services, therapy coverage, and access to wellness apps
- **Financial wellness programs**, including financial planning resources and retirement guidance, which are especially valuable in uncertain economic times
- **Flexible schedules and generous paid time off**
- **Physical wellness initiatives**, such as gym stipends or fitness programs
- **Family support**, including parental leave, childcare assistance, and eldercare resources

In a recession, when financial stress is high, these benefits matter even more. Employers that visibly care about their people's well-being earn loyalty that outlasts any single economic cycle.

## Strategy 6: Preserve Remote and Flexible Work

Some employers view a downturn as a chance to bring everyone back to the office. That could be a serious mistake.

For a large portion of the workforce, remote and hybrid work are now non-negotiable. Surveys consistently show that most employees who can work remotely want to keep doing so at least part of the time, and many say they would look for another job if flexibility were removed.

Remote work also has clear business advantages:

- **Access to a wider talent pool**, not limited by geography
- **Reduced overhead costs** for office space
- **Improved productivity and retention** for many roles
- **Greater diversity**, because location-based barriers disappear

Instead of rolling back flexibility, consider formalizing it with clear remote and hybrid policies, investing in collaboration tools, and training managers to lead distributed teams well. Flexibility is one of your strongest recruitment tools, and it costs far less than across-the-board raises.

## Preparing for the Talent Competition Ahead

A recession may slow hiring, but it won't reset workers' expectations or end the competition for skilled talent. The organizations that thrive will treat recruitment as a strategic priority rather than a transaction, adapting to what candidates want instead of waiting for the market to swing back.

HR teams that track economic data, invest in employee growth, stand for authentic values, adopt technology, support well-being, and keep flexibility in place can attract and retain top talent in any economic climate.
