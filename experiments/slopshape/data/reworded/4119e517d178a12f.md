# How to Get Your Doctor to Take Your Period Pain Seriously

If cramps have ever bent you double, kept you on the couch while you canceled plans, or landed you in a doctor's office hearing "that's just part of being a woman," many others have been there too. Your pain is real.

At Flex, we think open conversation about periods is how periods get better. Period pain has long been dismissed, minimized, or treated as something to endure in silence. That silence costs people: many menstruators wait years for answers, going from appointment to appointment until someone finally listens.

We asked two OB/GYNs, Dr. Charis Chambers and Dr. Laura Laursen, why period pain is so often underestimated and, above all, what you can do to make sure your doctor hears you.

## Why Period Pain Gets Underestimated

Some discomfort during your period is common. Common is not the same as normal, though, and it certainly doesn't mean you have to suffer.

One reason for the problem is that pain is subjective. No blood test measures how much your cramps hurt, so providers depend heavily on how patients describe it. Many of us also learned early to downplay period pain. We say "it's not that bad" when it is, and we keep going to work or school because we believe we should.

Dr. Chambers, known for her work educating people about periods, often stresses that pain that disrupts your life isn't something you should have to accept. When menstruators have been trained to minimize their symptoms, providers can end up with an incomplete picture.

History plays a part as well. Research has long shown that women's pain, and especially the pain of women of color, is more likely to be dismissed or undertreated in medical settings. Menstrual health has also historically received less research attention and less time in medical training than many other areas of health. Together, these factors produce a system in which period pain is easily dismissed.

## Why Birth Control Is Often the Default Answer

If you've raised painful periods with a doctor, you quite possibly left with a prescription for hormonal birth control. There is a reason for that.

For many people, hormonal birth control reduces period pain and heavy bleeding very effectively. It's widely available, well studied, and often a sensible first step.

As Dr. Chambers and Dr. Laursen both note, however, birth control can sometimes work like a Band-Aid, managing symptoms without finding their cause. If an underlying condition is behind your pain, birth control may hide it for a time without treating it. Some people also don't want to take hormones, can't tolerate them, or find they don't help.

You deserve to know *why* you hurt, not only how to dull the pain. You can ask your provider questions such as, "What might be causing this?" or "Are there tests we should consider?" or "What are my other options?"

## How to Prepare for Your Appointment

Preparation before an appointment can change how it goes. Our experts recommend the following.

### Describe How Pain Affects Your Life, Not Just a Number

You've likely been asked to rate your pain from 1 to 10. That number, however, means different things to different people. Your "6" could be another person's "9," particularly if you've lived with pain for years and learned to function through it.

Both doctors instead urge patients to describe how pain affects their daily life, which gives a provider information they can use. For example, you might say:

- "I miss one to two days of work every month because of my period."
- "I can't exercise or leave the house on the first day of my cycle."
- "The pain wakes me up at night."
- "I've gone to urgent care because of how much it hurt."
- "I have to plan my social life around my period."

Specific examples like these show clearly what you're going through.

### Log Exact Medication Doses and Timing

"I take ibuprofen" tells your doctor much less than "I take 400 mg of ibuprofen every four hours starting the day before my period, and it barely helps."

Keep track of:

- Which medications you take (over-the-counter or prescription).
- The exact dose.
- How often you take them.
- When you start taking them relative to your period.
- How much relief they give, if any.

This record shows your provider whether you've been using medications effectively and whether your pain responds to standard treatment. Pain that doesn't ease with appropriate doses of over-the-counter pain relievers is an important signal that deserves further investigation.

### Note Duration and Changes Over Time

How long does the pain last? Does it begin before your period? Does it continue after the bleeding stops? Has it worsened over the years?

Changes are particularly important to report. Pain that has steadily worsened, pain that's new, or pain that appears at other points in your cycle, such as during sex, bowel movements, or urination, can indicate specific conditions. Give as much detail as you can.

### Track Your Cycles

A period tracker, whether an app, a calendar, or a plain notebook, is one of the most useful tools you have. For a few months, record:

- When your period starts and ends.
- How heavy your flow is (and how often you need to change your pad, tampon, cup, or disc).
- When pain begins and how severe it is.
- Any other symptoms, like nausea, fatigue, bloating, headaches, or mood changes.

Bringing this record to your appointment reveals patterns that are hard to recall from memory, and it helps your provider take your concerns seriously.

## Conditions to Be Aware Of

Severe or worsening period pain can sometimes signal an underlying condition. Several are worth knowing about:

**Endometriosis:** Tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus. It can cause intense period pain, pain during sex, pain with bowel movements or urination, and fertility problems. Endometriosis is widely known to be underdiagnosed, and many people wait years for a diagnosis.

**Fibroids:** Noncancerous growths in or on the uterus. Fibroids can cause heavy or prolonged bleeding, pelvic pressure, and pain. They are very common and are diagnosed at higher rates among Black women.

**Adenomyosis:** The tissue lining the uterus grows into the uterus's muscular wall. It can cause heavy, painful periods and an enlarged, tender uterus.

If any of these sound familiar, bring them up with your provider and ask whether further evaluation, such as a pelvic exam, ultrasound, or referral to a specialist, could help.

## Advocating for Yourself

You may still feel unheard despite careful preparation. If so, you can keep asking questions. You can bring a friend or partner for support. You can ask your doctor to note in your chart that you requested further testing. You can also get a second opinion or look for a provider who specializes in menstrual and pelvic health.

Speaking up for yourself isn't being difficult. It's treating your health as seriously as your care team should.

## You Deserve to Be Heard

You don't have to endure period pain in silence. Your experience matters, your symptoms are valid, and you deserve answers. If you describe how pain affects your life, track your symptoms and medications, and ask careful questions, you can go into your next appointment prepared and confident.

At Flex, we'll keep talking about periods openly and honestly, because every menstruator deserves care that takes them seriously.
