# A Beginner's Guide to Common Gynecological Surgeries and Procedures

Plenty of people have sat in an exam room while a doctor used words like "oophorectomy" or "colposcopy" and come away confused. Gynecology has its own vocabulary, and hearing it applied to your own body and your own health decisions can be unnerving.

Many gynecological procedures are routine, quick, and low-risk. Some take less than half an hour in a clinic. Others are major surgeries that call for careful thought and a real conversation with your provider. This guide explains the most common ones in plain language, so you arrive at your next appointment better informed and less overwhelmed.

## What Is Gynecological Surgery?

The term covers any surgical procedure on the female reproductive system: the uterus, cervix, ovaries, fallopian tubes, vagina, and vulva. Surgeons perform these operations to diagnose a problem, treat a condition, relieve symptoms, prevent disease, or provide permanent contraception.

Most approaches fit into a few categories:

- **Open (abdominal) surgery**, performed through a larger incision in the lower abdomen.
- **Laparoscopic surgery**, performed through a few small incisions with a camera. Recovery is usually faster.
- **Robotic-assisted surgery**, a type of laparoscopy in which the surgeon operates precise robotic instruments.
- **Vaginal or hysteroscopic procedures**, which reach the reproductive organs through the vagina and cervix and often leave no external incisions.

The choice of approach depends on the condition, your anatomy, your medical history, and your surgeon's expertise.

## Oophorectomy

**What it is:** An oophorectomy removes one ovary (unilateral) or both (bilateral). Surgeons often take out the fallopian tubes at the same time, a combined operation called a salpingo-oophorectomy.

**Why it's done:** Typical reasons include ovarian cysts or tumors, ovarian torsion (an ovary twisting and cutting off its own blood supply), severe endometriosis, abscesses, and cancer. People with a high inherited risk of ovarian or breast cancer, such as carriers of BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations, may opt for a preventive oophorectomy.

**How it's performed:** Surgeons do most oophorectomies laparoscopically, but large masses or suspected cancer may require open surgery.

**Effects on fertility and hormones:** When one ovary comes out, the other usually keeps releasing eggs and making hormones, so fertility and hormonal function often remain intact. Removing both ovaries ends natural fertility and, if you haven't yet reached menopause, puts you into surgical menopause immediately. Expect possible hot flashes, sleep changes, and vaginal dryness, along with longer-term concerns for bone and heart health. Some people may be candidates for hormone therapy.

**Key risks:** Bleeding, infection, damage to nearby organs, and anesthesia complications. If both ovaries are removed before natural menopause, you and your provider should discuss the long-term health effects of losing estrogen early.

## Hysterectomy

**What it is:** A hysterectomy removes the uterus and ranks among the most common major surgeries women undergo in the United States. The main types are:

- **Total hysterectomy:** removes the uterus and cervix.
- **Partial (supracervical) hysterectomy:** removes the uterus but leaves the cervix.
- **Radical hysterectomy:** removes the uterus, cervix, part of the vagina, and surrounding tissue, usually to treat cancer.

The ovaries and tubes may or may not be removed as part of the operation.

**Why it's done:** Fibroids, heavy or abnormal bleeding, endometriosis, adenomyosis, uterine prolapse, chronic pelvic pain, and gynecologic cancers.

**How it's performed:** Through the vagina, laparoscopically, robotically, or through an abdominal incision. Minimally invasive methods usually shorten the hospital stay and cut recovery to a few weeks instead of six to eight.

**Effects on fertility and hormones:** Once the uterus is gone, your periods stop and pregnancy is no longer possible. Ovaries left in place generally keep producing hormones, so menopause doesn't start right away, although some research suggests it may come somewhat earlier than it otherwise would.

**Key risks:** Bleeding, infection, blood clots, bladder or bowel injury, and anesthesia reactions. The surgery is major and permanent, so ask whether a less invasive alternative could work for you.

## Fibroid Removal (Myomectomy)

**What it is:** Uterine fibroids are noncancerous growths in or on the uterus. A myomectomy takes out the fibroids and leaves the uterus in place.

**Why it's done:** Fibroids can cause heavy periods, pelvic pressure, pain, frequent urination, and fertility problems. People who want to keep their uterus or remain able to get pregnant often choose myomectomy.

**How it's performed:** The route depends on the fibroids' size, number, and location. Options include hysteroscopy (through the cervix, for fibroids inside the uterine cavity), laparoscopy, robotic surgery, or an abdominal incision.

**Effects on fertility and hormones:** The operation is meant to preserve fertility and leaves hormone production alone. If the surgery went deep into the uterine wall, your provider may suggest waiting a few months before trying to conceive and may recommend a cesarean delivery for future pregnancies.

**Key risks:** Bleeding (occasionally heavy), infection, scar tissue, and recurrence. Because new fibroids can develop over time, some people need more treatment later.

## Endometrial Ablation

**What it is:** Endometrial ablation destroys the uterine lining (the endometrium) to reduce or stop menstrual bleeding.

**Why it's done:** It mainly treats heavy menstrual bleeding in people who are done having children and haven't gotten relief from medication.

**How it's performed:** The procedure usually goes through the vagina and cervix without incisions. Providers may use heat, cold, radiofrequency energy, heated fluid, or microwave energy. Many ablations are outpatient procedures lasting under an hour, and most people resume normal activities within a few days.

**Effects on fertility and hormones:** Your ovaries and hormones are unaffected, but ablation is not for anyone who wants to get pregnant. Pregnancy afterward is uncommon but possible and carries serious risks, so you still need reliable contraception.

**Key risks:** Cramping, discharge, infection, and, in rare cases, uterine perforation or burns to nearby organs. In some people, bleeding returns over time.

## Tubal Ligation

**What it is:** Tubal ligation, commonly called "getting your tubes tied," is permanent birth control: the fallopian tubes are blocked, cut, or removed so that eggs and sperm can't meet. More and more surgeons now perform a **bilateral salpingectomy**, taking out the tubes entirely, which may also reduce the risk of certain ovarian cancers.

**Why it's done:** For people who are certain they don't want to become pregnant in the future.

**How it's performed:** Usually laparoscopically, through one or two small incisions, and often on an outpatient basis. It can also be done shortly after childbirth or during a cesarean section.

**Effects on fertility and hormones:** The procedure is meant to be permanent. Reversal is sometimes possible, but it is expensive, doesn't always work, and can't be done after a salpingectomy. Because the ovaries are untouched, your hormones and periods carry on as before. If your cycle does change, the cause may simply be that you stopped hormonal birth control.

**Key risks:** Anesthesia complications, infection, bleeding, and injury to nearby organs. A pregnancy that happens after the procedure is more likely to be ectopic, which is a medical emergency.

## Diagnostic and Minor Procedures

Many gynecological procedures happen outside the operating room. Quick in-office tests help your provider figure out what's going on.

### Colposcopy

During a colposcopy, your provider examines the cervix, vagina, and vulva through a magnifying instrument called a colposcope. It usually follows an abnormal Pap smear or a positive HPV test. The experience is similar to a Pap test, lasts about 10 to 20 minutes, and needs no anesthesia. Your provider may apply a vinegar-like solution to make abnormal cells stand out.

### Cervical Biopsy

If your provider sees something unusual during a colposcopy, they may take a small tissue sample from the cervix. You may feel brief cramping or a pinch, and light spotting afterward is common. Procedures such as a LEEP or cone biopsy remove larger areas of abnormal tissue and can double as treatment. They may slightly raise the risk of preterm birth in later pregnancies, so bring this up if you plan to conceive.

### Trachelectomy

A trachelectomy removes the cervix, usually to treat early-stage cervical cancer. A **radical trachelectomy** takes the cervix, surrounding tissue, and the upper vagina but keeps the uterus, which lets some patients preserve their fertility. Pregnancy afterward is possible but considered high-risk, and delivery is typically by cesarean. Unlike the other procedures in this section, a trachelectomy is major surgery.

### Cyst Removal (Ovarian Cystectomy)

Most ovarian cysts are harmless and resolve without treatment. A cyst that is large, painful, persistent, or suspicious on imaging may be removed in a cystectomy, usually done laparoscopically. The surgeon aims to remove the cyst and spare the healthy ovary, which protects hormone production and fertility. Recovery usually takes one to two weeks.

## Talk It Through With Your Provider

Knowing the vocabulary helps, but each body and situation is different. The best choice for you depends on your symptoms, your health history, your pregnancy plans, and your own priorities.

Before any procedure, consider asking:

- Why is this procedure recommended for me?
- Are there less invasive alternatives?
- How will this affect my fertility and hormones?
- What does recovery look like?
- What are the risks in my specific case?

You are entitled to clear answers and a care plan that suits your life. Ask questions, get a second opinion, or take time to decide. Reproductive health is personal, and the best decisions are informed ones.
