# 8 Real Upselling Examples from Brands That Get It Right (and What You Can Learn from Them)

I've been upsold more times than I can count. Sometimes I didn't notice. Sometimes I noticed, liked it, and paid more without complaint because the upgrade actually improved things for me. And a few times I left feeling I'd been talked into something I didn't need.

That mix is why upselling interests me so much. Handled well, it's one of the most customer-friendly ways to grow revenue. Handled badly, it leads quickly to buyer's remorse and churn.

Below are eight real upselling examples from familiar brands across SaaS, ecommerce, travel rewards, and subscriptions. For each, I'll explain the tactic, why it works, and what you can adapt for your own business. At the end, I'll show how contact scoring can tell you *who* is actually ready for an upsell, so you don't have to guess.

## What Is Upselling?

Upselling means encouraging a customer to buy a more expensive version of a product, an upgraded plan, or an add-on that raises the value of their purchase.

Cross-selling is different: it recommends *related* products ("You bought a camera, want a memory card?"). Upselling moves the customer up, to the bigger plan, the premium tier, or the deluxe package.

### Why Upselling Matters

The math is simple. Selling to someone who already knows and trusts you is usually much easier and cheaper than winning a brand-new customer. Existing customers have already made the hardest decision: that you're worth paying.

Upselling helps you:

- **Increase average order value and customer lifetime value.** Each customer is worth more over time.
- **Grow revenue without proportionally increasing acquisition costs.** You don't pay to find these people a second time.
- **Improve retention.** Customers who move to a plan that better fits their needs usually get more value from it, and those customers tend to stay.

There's an important condition, though: the upsell has to serve the customer. I'll return to this several times, because it separates upselling that builds loyalty from upselling that wears it down.

## 1. Highlighted Pricing Tiers: HubSpot

Anyone who has looked at a SaaS pricing page has almost certainly seen this. HubSpot's pricing pages display several tiers side by side and visually emphasize one, usually with a "Most Popular" label or a contrasting color.

When I first compared marketing tools, my eye went straight to the highlighted plan. I hadn't chosen to look there; the design steered me.

**Why it works:** A highlighted tier does two things. It eases decision fatigue by suggesting a "default" choice, and it applies social proof: if most people pick this plan, it's probably safe. It also anchors the options. Placed beside a much more expensive enterprise option, the highlighted middle tier looks very reasonable.

**What to borrow:** Highlight the plan that truly fits most of your customers, not just the priciest one. If people on the highlighted plan feel overcharged, you've exchanged short-term revenue for long-term frustration.

## 2. Defaulting to the Premium Option: Annual Plans and Pre-Selected Upgrades

Many subscription and SaaS companies, from Grammarly to Canva, configure checkout or pricing toggles so the premium or annual option is pre-selected. The upgraded choice is the default when the page loads, and you have to switch to the cheaper option yourself.

I've done exactly this with software subscriptions: the annual plan was toggled on, the savings were shown right beside it, and I simply accepted it. Most of the time I was glad I had.

**Why it works:** Defaults carry a lot of weight. People usually keep whatever is pre-selected, because changing it takes effort and a deliberate choice. When the default is also presented as a better deal (such as "Save 20% with annual billing"), it reads as the smart option rather than a sales push.

**What to borrow:** Choose defaults you can justify. Pre-select the option that gives the typical customer the best value, and make switching easy and obvious. Hiding the cheaper option or making it hard to find moves from persuasion to manipulation, and customers notice.

## 3. In-App Upgrade Prompts: Spotify

Spotify excels at the in-app upsell. Free users run into natural friction points: ads between songs, limited skips, no offline listening. At each one, Spotify reminds them, without pressure, that Premium removes the restriction.

I stayed on the free plan for a while. Then, on a long flight, I discovered I couldn't download my playlists and upgraded on the spot in the app. Spotify didn't have to persuade me at that point; the product already had.

**Why it works:** In-app prompts reach users at the exact moment they feel the problem the upgrade fixes. No long sales pitch is needed, because the value is immediate and obvious. The situation makes the sale.

**What to borrow:** Identify the points in your product where users hit limits. Those are the places for a well-timed, helpful prompt. Keep it friendly and easy to dismiss. If users feel nagged or blocked at every step, the prompts come across as punishment rather than help.

## 4. Custom Enterprise Plans: Slack

Slack sells standard self-serve plans, but the top of its pricing page shows an enterprise option with a "Contact Sales" call to action in place of a fixed price. That tier includes advanced security, compliance features, and support built for large organizations.

**Why it works:** Large companies have complex requirements, such as security reviews, data residency rules, and custom integrations, that a standard plan can't easily accommodate. A custom enterprise plan tells them, "We can handle your scale." It also starts a sales conversation in which the team can shape the offer and pricing around the customer's real requirements.

I've sat on those calls as the buyer. The good ones worked because the sales rep asked about our needs before pitching. The bad ones sounded scripted.

**What to borrow:** If your customers vary widely in size, consider a custom top tier. Use the sales conversation to understand the customer's challenges, not only to close. The more closely your enterprise offer matches what they need, the easier the upgrade becomes.

## 5. Discounted Upgrades for Past Buyers: Apple Trade In

Apple's trade-in program lets existing customers swap an older device for credit toward a new one. Owners of an aging iPhone can easily apply its value to the latest model.

When I last upgraded my phone, the trade-in credit decided it. I hadn't planned to buy that year, but the lower effective price made the upgrade seem like a sensible step rather than a splurge.

**Why it works:** Past buyers already trust the brand and know the product. A discount or credit toward an upgrade rewards their loyalty and makes spending more easier. It also keeps them in the ecosystem rather than losing them to a competitor.

**What to borrow:** Review your past customers and think about what a loyalty-based upgrade offer could be: a discount on the next tier, credit for their current plan, or an exclusive upgrade price. Show them you recognize that they came back.

## 6. Recommendation Feeds: Amazon

Amazon's recommendation engine is famous. While you browse, you see sections like "Customers also bought," "Compare with similar items," and suggestions for higher-end versions of the item in front of you. A premium alternative with better reviews or more features often sits only a few dollars away.

I once set out to buy a basic pair of headphones and bought a clearly better pair after the product comparison showed what a little more money would get me. I didn't feel pushed; I felt informed.

**Why it works:** Recommendation feeds draw on browsing and purchase data to show relevant upgrades. Because the suggestions are personalized and framed as helpful comparisons, they seem like shopping advice rather than an upsell.

**What to borrow:** This doesn't require Amazon's data science team. Simple logic, such as showing a premium version next to a standard product or adding a comparison table, can steer customers toward better options. Relevance is what matters: recommend upgrades that fit what the customer is trying to accomplish.

## 7. Well-Timed Email Offers: Airline and Hotel Upgrade Emails

Travel brands are very good at timing upsell emails. After a flight is booked, many airlines send emails in the days before departure offering seat upgrades, extra legroom, or a chance to bid on a premium cabin seat. Hotel loyalty programs like Marriott Bonvoy do much the same, offering room upgrades or add-ons ahead of your stay.

An upgrade email that arrives the night before a long-haul flight is hard to resist. I've accepted more than one because the timing was ideal: I was already thinking about the trip, and the upgrade suddenly seemed concrete and worth it.

**Why it works:** Timing decides it. The customer has already committed to the purchase, is looking forward to the experience, and receives the offer just when the value of an upgrade is on their mind. It reads as an opportunity, not a pitch.

**What to borrow:** Identify the points in your customer journey where an upgrade feels most relevant: right after onboarding, when a customer reaches a usage milestone, before a renewal, or just before a key event. Use email automation to send offers at those points instead of emailing everyone at once.

## 8. VIP Memberships: Amazon Prime and Sephora Beauty Insider

VIP memberships make upselling an ongoing relationship. Amazon Prime combines fast shipping, streaming, and exclusive deals in one membership. Sephora's Beauty Insider program places customers in tiers that unlock perks, such as birthday gifts, exclusive events, and bigger rewards, as their spending grows.

I've belonged to both kinds of programs, and the perks really do change how I shop. After I got Prime, I stopped checking shipping costs elsewhere. With tiered rewards programs, I've clearly felt drawn to reach the next level.

**Why it works:** Memberships give customers a sense of belonging and exclusivity. They also change how customers think: having paid for (or earned) VIP status, they want to get full use of it, which leads to repeat purchases and deeper engagement.

**What to borrow:** Decide whether a membership or tiered loyalty program suits your business. The perks can be simple. Early access, exclusive content, priority support, or member-only pricing can all deliver real value. Just make sure the benefits are genuine and easy to understand.

## A Word of Caution: Don't Push Customers Beyond Their Budget

Many of these examples worked well for me as a buyer. I've also had the reverse: pressure into a plan I didn't need, features I never used, and finally a cancellation out of frustration.

Having been on both sides, here's what I've learned:

- **Upsell to fit, not to maximize.** The best upsell matches the customer's real needs. If they don't need the enterprise plan, don't push it.
- **Respect budgets.** A customer stretched past what they can comfortably afford is likely to churn, ask for refunds, or leave a negative review.
- **Make downgrading and saying "no" easy.** Customers trust you more, not less, when they know they aren't trapped.
- **Be transparent.** Clear pricing and honest comparisons build credibility, while hidden fees and confusing terms destroy it.

The aim isn't to extract every possible dollar from one transaction. It's to build a relationship in which customers willingly grow with you over time.

## How Contact Scoring Helps You Upsell at the Right Moment

All of these examples share one trait: the upsell succeeds because it reaches the right person at the right time. Spotify's prompt appears when a user hits a limit. The airline email arrives just before the flight. Apple's trade-in offer targets people with an older device.

For most sales and marketing teams, the hardest part is knowing who is ready for an upsell. Your database is full of customers, but which of them are ready to upgrade, and which would be irritated by the pitch?

Contact scoring answers that question.

It gives each contact a score based on two factors:

- **Fit:** How closely does this contact match your ideal customer profile for the upgrade? Consider company size, industry, role, or current plan.
- **Activity:** How is the contact engaging with you? Are they logging in more often, hitting usage limits, visiting your pricing page, opening upgrade emails, or attending webinars about advanced features?

Together, fit and activity show which customers are most likely ready for an upsell. Rather than sending one upgrade email to everyone, your team can concentrate on contacts showing real buying signals and contact them when the offer is most relevant.
