# "Price Should Follow Value, Not Your Costs": Nicolas Dessaigne on Pricing, Billing, and Open Source

Nicolas Dessaigne has spent more time than most founders inside the awkward transition to usage-based pricing. As co-founder and former CEO of Algolia, he helped grow a small search engine for mobile apps into the search and discovery API that thousands of websites and apps rely on, serving billions of queries a month. Over that period, Algolia replaced its simple fixed plans with a model that charged for usage: records indexed and search operations performed.

On a slide, the switch looks tidy. In reality it reached into sales, finance, and the knot of code that converted usage data into invoices. We asked Nicolas what worked, what didn't, and what he would tell founders now.

---

**Let's start at the beginning. How did you pick your first plans?**

Honestly, we copied the shape of what other developer tools offered and took an educated guess: a handful of tiers, each with a monthly price and a cap on records and operations. Keeping it simple was correct. With ten customers, a buyer has to grasp what they're paying for in roughly five seconds.

The catch is that those early plans outlive your expectations. You tell yourself you'll revisit them in six months, and three years on you're still supporting them.

**Why did you decide to build billing in-house?**

It looked easy, and that's exactly the trap. In the early days, billing amounts to a cron job that tallies usage and passes a number to Stripe. An engineer can write it in a week. We were an engineering-led company, so building things ourselves was our default. We wanted control, and nothing on the market fit the way we wanted to charge.

So we wrote it, it worked, and for a while nobody gave it another thought.

**You've mentioned a pricing experiment that didn't go the way you hoped. What happened?**

At one point we tried to link our prices more tightly to our running costs. From inside the company the reasoning seemed airtight: some operations were expensive for our infrastructure, so they should cost customers more. We reorganized part of the pricing on that basis.

Customers hated it. Price wasn't really the complaint; the structure made no sense from their side. Our server costs meant nothing to them. What they cared about was what search did for their business: conversions, engagement, happy users. To predict a bill, they would have had to understand our architecture, and that's an awful experience to put someone through.

We reversed much of it. The lesson hurt, but it was a valuable one.

**What was the lesson, in a sentence?**

Price should follow the value your customer gets, not your internal costs. Costs define the floor, and your margins have to hold up. The metric on the invoice, though, should be one the customer sees as value. When they grow and succeed, your revenue should rise alongside them, and that should feel good to them rather than come as a surprise.

**Let's go back to the homegrown billing system. When did it start to hurt?**

Gradually. Finance came first with its requirements: proper revenue recognition, invoices that matched contracts, credit notes, taxes across countries. Next, sales began closing custom deals with commitments, discounts, and overage rates, and each one needed its own special case in the code.

Grandfathered plans came after that. Whenever we changed pricing, we left existing customers on their old plans, which is the right move for trust. The consequence was five, six, seven generations of pricing running at once, and the billing logic had to get every one of them right.

Then came fundraising. In a big round, investors want a deep view of your revenue: net dollar retention, expansion by cohort, usage trends. At that point billing data stops being only about invoices and becomes the source of truth for how you present the company. Messy data there is a serious problem.

**So what's your advice to founders deciding whether to build or buy?**

Unless billing is your product, don't build it. I understand the pull, particularly for technical founders: it looks simple, and building it seems to promise more flexibility. But the first version isn't where the complexity lives. It shows up in year two, year three, year five, when finance, sales, and investors bring requirements you can't picture today.

My second piece of advice is to choose something that won't box you in. Your pricing will change, probably many times, and your tool should let you experiment without a rewrite each time.

**Why do you think open source matters for something like billing?**

Billing is extremely sensitive, because it holds both your revenue and your customers' trust. Open source lets you see precisely how each amount is calculated. You can self-host if you need to. You can extend it when your model is unusual, and with usage-based pricing it nearly always is. Launching a new plan doesn't depend on a vendor's roadmap.

It also takes away the fear of lock-in. Few projects a company can take on are as frightening as migrating billing systems.

**And how do open-core companies actually make money if the code is free?**

The free core is what drives adoption: developers try it, come to trust it, and build on it. As companies grow, they need things that matter at scale, such as a managed cloud so they don't have to operate it themselves, enterprise features for security and permissions, advanced integrations, and support backed by real guarantees. Those are what they pay for.

The strongest open-core companies are very honest about where that line sits. The free version must be genuinely useful rather than a crippled demo. Get that right, and your community turns into your best sales channel.
